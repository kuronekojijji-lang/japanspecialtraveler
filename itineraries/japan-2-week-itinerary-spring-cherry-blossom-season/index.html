Japan Spring Cherry Blossom: 14-Day Adventure

Last updated: March 30, 2026

The Ultimate Japan 2 Week Itinerary for Spring Cherry Blossom Season (2025 Edition)

If you’ve dreamed of standing beneath a canopy of pale pink petals while ancient temples rise behind you, this Japan 2 week itinerary spring cherry blossom season guide will turn that dream into a concrete, day-by-day plan. After living in Japan for fifteen years, I’ve fine-tuned this route through countless springs — adjusting for bloom timing, crowd patterns, regional food seasons, and those quiet moments that transform a good trip into an unforgettable one.

This isn’t a generic highlight reel. It’s a carefully sequenced two-week journey that follows the cherry blossoms as they move across the country, weaves in seasonal spring cuisine at every stop, and balances iconic sights with off-the-beaten-path discoveries that most visitors never find. Whether you’re a first-timer or returning for your second or third visit, this itinerary gives you the Japan that locals experience in spring — vivid, fleeting, and deeply beautiful.

Meta description: A detailed Japan 2 week itinerary for spring cherry blossom season covering Tokyo, Hakone, Kyoto, Nara, Osaka, Hiroshima, Miyajima, and Takayama — with seasonal food, local tips, and day-by-day planning.

Who This Itinerary Is For

This route works best for:

  • First-time visitors who want the essential Japan experience elevated by cherry blossom season
  • Couples and solo travelers looking for a mix of culture, nature, and incredible food
  • Returning visitors who came in autumn or summer and want to see Japan’s most magical season
  • Moderate walkers — most days involve 12,000–18,000 steps, but the pace is balanced with rest days

We’ll cover Tokyo → Hakone → Kyoto → Nara → Osaka → Hiroshima & Miyajima → Takayama → Tokyo, timed so you’re in the right place as the blossoms open.

Day-by-Day Itinerary

Day 1: Arrival in Tokyo — Shinjuku Gyoen and First Blossoms

What to see: After landing at Narita or Haneda, head to your hotel to drop bags, then make Shinjuku Gyoen your first stop. This is my single favorite cherry blossom spot in Tokyo — 1,100 cherry trees across 58 hectares, with varieties that bloom at different times, meaning you’re almost guaranteed blossoms whenever you visit between late March and mid-April. The garden closes at 4:30 PM (last entry 4:00 PM), so aim to arrive by 2:00 PM. Alcohol is prohibited inside, making it calmer than public parks.

In the evening, walk through Shinjuku’s neon corridors and explore Omoide Yokocho (“Memory Lane”) — a narrow alley of tiny yakitori joints that feels like stepping into 1960s Tokyo.

What to eat: Start with a bowl of shio ramen at Fuunji in Shinjuku (the tsukemen is legendary — expect a 20-minute queue). For dinner, grab yakitori skewers at Omoide Yokocho. Order the negima (chicken and leek) and tsukune (chicken meatball).

Where to stay: Shinjuku — excellent transit hub for the entire Tokyo portion. I recommend the Shinjuku Granbell Hotel or, for budget travelers, Unplan Shinjuku.

Day 2: Tokyo — Ueno, Asakusa, and the Sumida River

What to see: Ueno Park is Tokyo’s most famous hanami (flower viewing) spot, with over 800 cherry trees lining the main walkway. Get here by 9:00 AM to enjoy it before the picnic tarps completely take over. Stroll through to the Tokyo National Museum if you want a cultural anchor.

After lunch, walk or take the Ginza Line to Asakusa. Senso-ji temple is gorgeous framed by blossoms, but the real magic is a Sumida River cruise — the river-facing cherry trees along Sumida Park create one of Tokyo’s most photogenic spring scenes. The water bus to Odaiba costs about ¥1,200 and takes 40 minutes.

What to eat: In Ueno, try sakura mochi (pink rice cake wrapped in a pickled cherry leaf) from any vendor — it’s THE seasonal sweet of spring. In Asakusa, have lunch at Sometaro, a charming old-house restaurant where you make your own okonomiyaki on a tabletop griddle.

Where to stay: Shinjuku (same hotel).

Day 3: Tokyo — Meiji Shrine, Harajuku, and Meguro River at Night

What to see: Start the morning at Meiji Shrine — the towering torii gate and forest feel sacred even with crowds. Then walk through Harajuku’s Takeshita Street for the culture shock, and explore the more refined Omotesando avenue (Tokyo’s Champs-Élysées).

Afternoon: Visit teamLab Borderless at Azabudai Hills (opened 2024 — book tickets online at least a week ahead).

Evening: The Meguro River is the most spectacular nighttime cherry blossom viewing in Tokyo. Roughly 800 trees line both banks, and they’re illuminated from dusk until 9:00 PM. Walk from Nakameguro Station south along the river. The combination of lanterns, petals on the water, and small wine bars along the banks is intoxicating. Peak bloom here is typically March 28–April 7.

What to eat: Lunch at Afuri in Harajuku — their yuzu shio ramen is a spring essential, light and citrusy. Along the Meguro River, grab a sakura latte or hanami beer from the pop-up stalls that appear during blossom season.

Where to stay: Shinjuku (same hotel).

Day 4: Hakone Day Trip — Hot Springs and Mountain Views

What to see: Take the Romancecar from Shinjuku Station (85 minutes, reserve window seats on the left side for mountain views). In Hakone, follow the classic loop: Hakone Open Air Museum → ropeway over Owakudani volcanic valley → pirate ship across Lake Ashi with potential Fuji views.

End the day at a ryokan or day-use onsen. Tenzan Toji-kyo is a superb public outdoor onsen complex that’s popular with locals — nothing like soaking in mineral-rich water surrounded by spring greenery.

What to eat: At Owakudani, eat the famous kuro-tamago (black eggs boiled in volcanic sulfur springs) — legend says each one adds seven years to your life. For a proper lunch, try Hakone’s specialty: tofu kaiseki (multi-course tofu meal) at Hatsuhana.

Where to stay: Return to Shinjuku in the evening, OR splurge on one night at a Hakone ryokan (Fukuzumiro is historic and reasonably priced).

Day 5: Shinkansen to Kyoto — Afternoon on the Philosopher’s Path

What to see: Take the morning Nozomi shinkansen from Tokyo Station to Kyoto (2 hours 15 minutes). Sit on the right side (seat E) for views of Mt. Fuji around 40 minutes in — clear mornings give you about a 2-minute window.

After checking in, head to the Philosopher’s Path (Tetsugaku no Michi). This 2-kilometer canal-side walk lined with hundreds of cherry trees is Kyoto’s most romantic spring stroll. Start from Ginkaku-ji (Silver Pavilion) and walk south. The late afternoon light filtering through blossoms here is extraordinary. Peak bloom in Kyoto is typically April 1–10, though it has been trending a few days earlier in recent years.

What to eat: Near the Philosopher’s Path, stop at Omen for handmade udon noodles served with seasonal vegetables — a Kyoto institution. For dinner, try obanzai (Kyoto home-style cooking) at any small restaurant in the Gion area.

Where to stay: Downtown Kyoto, near Karasuma-Oike or Gion-Shijo Station. Hotel Anteroom Kyoto or Piece Hostel Sanjo (budget) are excellent choices.

Day 6: Kyoto — Arashiyama in Full Bloom

What to see: Take the JR Sagano Line to Arashiyama early (arrive by 8:00 AM — this is non-negotiable for crowd management). Walk through the bamboo grove first, before the crowds arrive, then explore Tenryu-ji temple’s garden with its weeping cherry trees.

Cross the iconic Togetsukyo Bridge — the mountains behind it turn soft pink and green in spring, creating one of the most photographed scenes in all of Japan. Rent a rowboat on the Hozu River (¥1,500 for 30 minutes) for a completely different perspective on the blossoms.

Afternoon: Visit the lesser-known Ninna-ji Temple, famous for its late-blooming Omuro cherry trees — these are short, multi-petaled trees that bloom about a week after Kyoto’s main peak, so even if you’ve missed peak bloom elsewhere, Ninna-ji often delivers.

What to eat: In Arashiyama, try yudofu (simmered tofu) — it sounds simple, but the silky Kyoto tofu is a completely different experience from what you know. Sagano is a classic spot overlooking a garden. Also try warabi mochi (bracken starch jelly with kinako powder) from street vendors — it’s a quintessential spring sweet.

Where to stay: Kyoto (same hotel).

Day 7: Kyoto — Fushimi Inari, Higashiyama, and Maruyama Park at Night

What to see: Start at Fushimi Inari at 7:00 AM — the thousands of vermillion torii gates are magical in morning light, and you’ll have portions of the trail almost to yourself. You don’t need to climb all the way to the summit; the midpoint lookout is satisfying.

Afternoon: Walk through the beautifully preserved Higashiyama district — Ninenzaka and Sannenzaka lanes are lined with traditional shops. Visit Kodai-ji temple for its shidarezakura (weeping cherry trees).

Evening: Maruyama Park’s massive weeping cherry tree is illuminated at night and is the heart of Kyoto’s hanami culture. Bring a convenience store bento and sit on a blue tarp like locals do. The atmosphere — families, couples, salarymen singing — is pure joy.

What to eat: Near Fushimi Inari, eat inari sushi (sweet tofu pockets stuffed with rice) — you’re at the shrine that gives this dish its name. In Higashiyama, stop at Kasagi-ya for matcha and Japanese sweets. For dinner, splurge on Kyoto-style kaiseki — Giro Giro Hitoshina makes the elaborate multi-course format accessible and fun (reservations essential, around ¥6,000 per person).

Where to stay: Kyoto (same hotel).

Day 8: Day Trip to Nara (and Optional Yoshino Mountain)

What to see: Nara is 45 minutes from Kyoto by Kintetsu Railway. The 1,200 free-roaming deer in Nara Park are endlessly entertaining, especially when combined with cherry blossoms. Visit Todai-ji (the world’s largest wooden building and its massive bronze Buddha), then wander to the quieter Yoshikien Garden.

For ambitious travelers: Continue from Nara to Yoshino Mountain (1 hour by Kintetsu), Japan’s single most famous cherry blossom site. 30,000 trees cover the mountainside in layers — lower, middle, upper, and inner — blooming in succession from early to mid-April. It’s crowded on weekends but transformative on weekdays.

What to eat: Nara’s specialty is kakinoha sushi — sushi wrapped in persimmon leaves, which impart a subtle, unique flavor. Hirasou near Nara Park is the place to try it. Also try kuzu mochi (kudzu starch dessert), a Yoshino specialty if you make the trip.

Where to stay: Kyoto (same hotel — return by evening).

Day 9: Kyoto — Kiyomizu-dera, Nishiki Market, and Tea Ceremony

What to see: Visit Kiyomizu-dera temple by 8:00 AM (it opens at 6:00 AM — use this to your advantage). The massive wooden terrace overlooking Kyoto, backed by cherry blossoms, is one of Japan’s most iconic views.

Late morning: Nishiki Market — Kyoto’s “Kitchen” — stretches for five blocks and is packed with seasonal specialties. Afternoon: Book a tea ceremony experience (I recommend Camellia near Kennin-ji — English-friendly, intimate, and authentic, around ¥3,000).

What to eat: At Nishiki, graze your way through: dashimaki tamago (rolled omelet), tsukemono (Kyoto pickles — try the seasonal sakura daikon), fresh yuba (tofu skin), and matcha soft serve. For a proper lunch, try nishin soba (herring on buckwheat noodles) at Matsuba — a Kyoto specialty since 1861.

Where to stay: Kyoto (same hotel — last night).

Day 10: Travel to Osaka — Dotonbori and Street Food Heaven

What to see: Kyoto to Osaka is only 15 minutes by shinkansen or 30 minutes by Hankyu Railway. Drop bags and dive into Osaka, a city that runs on appetite. Walk Dotonbori — the neon-drenched canal street — and explore the backstreets of Namba.

Afternoon: Visit Osaka’s Kema Sakuranomiya Park along the O River — 4,500 cherry trees line the riverbanks and it’s far less crowded than Kyoto’s spots.

What to eat: Osaka is Japan’s street food capital. Your essential checklist: takoyaki (octopus balls) at Wanaka, okonomiyaki (savory pancake) at Mizuno, kushikatsu (deep-fried skewers) at Daruma in Shinsekai. Osaka locals eat like it’s a competitive sport — embrace it.

Where to stay: Namba or Shinsaibashi area. Cross Hotel Osaka is stylish and central. Budget option: The Stay Osaka Shinsaibashi.

Day 11: Day Trip to Hiroshima and Miyajima Island

What to see: Take the morning shinkansen from Shin-Osaka to Hiroshima (80 minutes). Visit the Peace Memorial Park and Museum — allow at least 90 minutes for the museum, which was beautifully renovated in 2019. It’s emotionally heavy but essential.

After lunch, take the JR ferry to Miyajima Island (25 minutes from Hiroshima). The floating torii gate of Itsukushima Shrine at high tide, framed by cherry blossoms on the hillside, is one of Japan’s most sacred images. Check tide times in advance at itsukushima.net.

What to eat: Hiroshima-style okonomiyaki is layered (not mixed) and includes noodles — completely different from Osaka’s version. Try Nagata-ya near Peace Park. On Miyajima, eat momiji manju (maple leaf-shaped cakes filled with red bean, custard, or chocolate) fresh from the oven, and grilled anago (conger eel) — Miyajima’s signature dish.

Where to stay: Return to Osaka by evening (same hotel).

Day 12: Osaka — Osaka Castle and Shinsekai

What to see: Osaka Castle is surrounded by 3,000 cherry trees in Nishinomaru Garden (¥350 entry) — the white castle tower rising above pink blossoms is an unmissable photograph. Visit in the morning for softer light.

Afternoon: Explore Shinsekai, Osaka’s retro entertainment district — it feels like a time capsule from the 1950s. If you have energy, visit the Umeda Sky Building’s rooftop observatory at sunset.

What to eat: For lunch, try kitsune udon (fat udon noodles with sweet fried tofu) — Osaka claims to have invented it. Usami Tei Matsubaya has been serving it since 1893. In Shinsekai, it’s kushikatsu again — the neighborhood rule is “no double-dipping” in the communal sauce.

Where to stay: Osaka (same hotel — last night).

Day 13: Travel to Takayama — A Mountain Detour

What to see: This is the itinerary’s wildcard, and I include it every time because travelers consistently rate it as their trip highlight. Take the shinkansen from Osaka to Nagoya (50 minutes), then the JR Wide View Hida limited express to Takayama (2.5 hours of spectacular mountain scenery).

Takayama’s Sanmachi Suji old town district feels like stepping into the Edo period — dark-timbered merchant houses, sake breweries with sugidama (cedar balls) hanging outside, and a pace of life that’s blissfully slow. Cherry blossoms here bloom about 1–2 weeks later than Kyoto (mid-to-late April), so this can be your “second chance” for peak bloom.

Visit at least two sake breweries — Funasaka and Harada are my favorites. Tasting sets are usually ¥300–500 for three varieties.

What to eat: Takayama’s food is mountain cuisine: Hida beef (rivaling Kobe beef at half the price — get it grilled on a hoba magnolia leaf as hoba miso), Takayama ramen (thin curly noodles in a soy-based broth), and mitarashi dango (soy-glazed rice dumplings grilled over charcoal). Visit Kin no Kotte Ushi for incredible Hida beef sushi served on a rice cracker — ¥1,000 for two pieces of pure bliss.

Where to stay: Takayama — try a traditional ryokan like Oyado Koto no Yume (includes a wonderful dinner and breakfast). Budget option: Guesthouse Tomaru.

Day 14: Takayama Morning Market and Return to Tokyo

What to see: Takayama’s morning markets (asa-ichi) run along the Miyagawa River from 7:00 AM. Local farmers sell pickles, handcrafts, fresh produce, and seasonal treats. It’s a gentle, beautiful way to spend your last morning.

Take the late morning Hida express to Nagoya, then shinkansen to Tokyo (total about 4.5 hours). Spend your final evening in Tokyo — I’d suggest a last sunset walk at Chidorigafuchi, the moat around the Imperial Palace where cherry trees form a tunnel over rented rowboats.

What to eat: At Takayama’s morning market, try gohei mochi (pounded rice on a stick glazed with miso-walnut sauce). Back in Tokyo, have a farewell dinner at an intimate izakaya — Shirube in Ebisu is tiny, friendly, and serves perfect seasonal small plates.

Where to stay: Near Tokyo Station or Shinjuku for easy airport access the next morning.

Food Highlights: Spring Seasonal and Regional Dishes on This Route

Spring eating in Japan is an experience unto itself. Here are the can’t-miss dishes, organized by location:

Seasonal (available everywhere in spring):

  • Sakura mochi — pink rice cake in a pickled cherry leaf
  • Takenoko (bamboo shoots) — at peak freshness in April, often in tempura or rice dishes
  • Sakura-ebi (tiny pink shrimp) — harvested from Suruga Bay, served raw or as kakiage tempura
  • Spring sansai (mountain vegetables) — tempura of fukinoto, kogomi, and tara no me
  • Hanami bento — cherry blossom viewing lunchboxes from department store basements (depachika)

Regional:

  • Tokyo: Tsukemen, yakitori, monjayaki
  • Hakone: Kuro-tamago, tofu kaiseki
  • Kyoto: Yudofu, obanzai, nishin soba, yuba, matcha everything
  • Nara: Kakinoha sushi, kuzu mochi
  • Osaka: Takoyaki, okonomiyaki, kushikatsu, kitsune udon
  • Hiroshima: Hiroshima-style okonomiyaki, Miyajima anago
  • Takayama: Hida beef, hoba miso, Takayama ramen, mitarashi dango

Pro tip: Department store basement food halls (depachika) are Japan’s greatest food secret. Visit Isetan Shinjuku, Daimaru Kyoto, or Hanshin Umeda for exquisitely prepared seasonal foods at reasonable prices.

Transport Guide: Getting Between Cities

JR Pass Recommendation

For this itinerary, a 14-day Japan Rail Pass (Ordinary) at ¥50,000 (2024 pricing) is worthwhile. It covers:

  • Tokyo ↔ Kyoto shinkansen (Hikari — not Nozomi)
  • Kyoto ↔ Osaka local JR trains
  • Osaka ↔ Hiroshima shinkansen
  • Hiroshima ↔ Miyajima JR ferry
  • Osaka ↔ Nagoya shinkansen
  • Nagoya ↔ Takayama limited express
  • Takayama ↔ Tokyo (via Nagoya)

Important: The JR Pass does NOT cover the Nozomi or Mizuho shinkansen. Use the Hikari (only 10 minutes slower on most routes). Activate your pass on Day 5 when you leave for Kyoto.

For Days 1–4 in Tokyo and Hakone, purchase individual tickets or a Suica/Pasmo IC card.

IC Cards

Get a Suica or Pasmo card (or use the Suica app on iPhone) immediately on arrival. It works on virtually all trains, buses, and convenience stores across Japan. Load ¥3,000–5,000 to start.

Tips for This Season: Spring Cherry Blossom Weather, Crowds, and Booking

Bloom Timing (Average — Check jnto.go.jp/sakura for real-time updates)

Location Typical Peak Bloom
Tokyo March 28 – April 5
Kyoto April 1 – April 10
Osaka March 30 – April 7
Hiroshima March 28 – April 5
Takayama April 12 – April 20
Yoshino April 5 – April 15 (varies by elevation)

Key insight: Climate change has been pushing bloom dates earlier — in 2023, Tokyo peaked on March 22. Follow @sakura_forecast on Twitter and check the Japan Meteorological Corporation’s (JMC) forecast starting in January.

Weather

  • Late March: 10–17°C, cool mornings, occasional rain. Pack layers.
  • Early April: 13–20°C, pleasant but variable. A light rain jacket is essential.
  • Mid-April: 15–22°C, warming up, beautiful but some blossoms falling.

What to pack: Light layers, a compact umbrella, comfortable walking shoes (you’ll walk 15–25km daily), and a small portable battery pack. Coin lockers are everywhere for shedding layers.

Crowd Management

  • Visit temples before 8:30 AM or after 4:00 PM — midday is chaos at popular spots
  • Weekdays vs. weekends: The difference is enormous. If you can arrange travel days on weekends and sightseeing on weekdays, do it.
  • Golden Week (April 29–May 5) — avoid this period entirely. Domestic travel surges, prices spike, and everything is booked.
  • Book all shinkansen reserved seats during cherry blossom season, especially on Friday/Sunday

The “Cherry Blossom Panic”

First-timers often stress about missing the exact peak. Here’s the truth: a full bloom ±5 days is still spectacular. Even sakura fubuki (petal blizzards) during the days after peak are incredibly beautiful — some locals actually prefer this stage. And with this itinerary covering locations that bloom at different times, you’re virtually guaranteed stunning blossoms somewhere on your route.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What are the best dates to arrive in Japan for cherry blossom season?

Aim for late March to early April for the highest probability of catching blossoms in Tokyo and Kyoto. Specifically, arriving March 27–April 2 has historically given the best odds across multiple cities. Check bloom forecasts starting in February to fine-tune your dates.

2. Is 2 weeks enough for a Japan 2 week itinerary spring cherry blossom season trip?

Absolutely — 14 days is the sweet spot. It’s long enough to cover Tokyo, Kyoto, Osaka, Hiroshima, and a mountain town without feeling rushed, but tight enough to maintain energy and excitement. Any shorter and you’d need to cut meaningful experiences.

3. Should I buy the JR Pass for this itinerary?

Yes. The long-distance shinkansen rides alone (Tokyo–Kyoto round trip, Osaka–Hiroshima round trip, Osaka–Takayama–Tokyo routing) exceed ¥70,000 in individual ticket costs, making the ¥50,000 14-day pass a clear win. Activate it on your first long-distance travel day.

4. How do I handle luggage when moving between cities?

Use Japan’s takkyubin (luggage forwarding) service. Send your bags from your Tokyo hotel to your Kyoto hotel for about ¥2,000–2,500 per suitcase. Yamato Transport (look for the black cat logo) operates counters at most hotels and convenience stores. Bags typically arrive the next day. This lets you ride the shinkansen hands-free.

5. Is Japan expensive during cherry blossom season?

Accommodation prices in Kyoto increase 30–50% during peak bloom, and popular ryokan sell out entirely. However, food, transport, and activities remain the same price year-round. Budget travelers can still manage on $100–120/day per person by staying in hostels and eating at ramen shops, conveyor-belt sushi, and convenience stores.

6. What if I miss the cherry blossoms entirely?

Even if unseasonable weather disrupts the bloom, this itinerary is packed with incredible temples, food experiences, and cultural moments that stand on their own. Japan in spring is beautiful regardless of blossoms — the weather is mild, the seasonal food is at its best, and the Japanese excitement about spring is infectious. Plus, late-blooming varieties at places like Ninna-ji and Takayama extend the window significantly.
