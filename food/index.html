Sushi

Fresh fish · Vinegared rice · Omakase

Sushi was born in Tokyo (then Edo) as fast street food — fresh fish pressed onto vinegared rice, served at the counter. Today, from ¥150-a-piece standing bars to ¥50,000 omakase counters, Japan offers the widest sushi spectrum on earth. The golden rule: always eat closest to where the fish lands.

Where to eat the best sushi in Japan

Edo-mae Sushi Tokyo Tsukiji Outer Market, Ginza & the world's most Michelin-starred sushi counters
→
Sea of Japan Sushi Ishikawa Kanazawa's Omicho Market — crab, buri, and Sea of Japan fish at peak freshness
→
Uni & Ikura Sushi Hokkaido Hakodate morning market — fresh uni, ikura & crab eaten at sunrise
→
Tachigui Standing Sushi Osaka Kuromon Ichiba & standing sushi bars — point, receive, eat, no menu needed
→
Kaisen & Oysters Miyagi Matsushima oysters & Sendai kaisen-don — Pacific seafood at its finest
→
Sushi × Sake Pairing Niigata Premium sushi rice & local sake — the region where both are at their peak
→

Ramen

Broth · Noodles · Regional styles

Ramen is Japan's national comfort food — and contrary to popular belief, every region has its own style, broth, and toppings. Miso in Sapporo, tonkotsu in Hakata, shoyu in Tokyo, shio in Hakodate: the four great base styles spawn hundreds of local variations.

Japan's great regional ramen styles

Sapporo Miso Ramen Hokkaido Rich miso broth, roasted corn, butter & menma — born in Sapporo in 1955
→
Hakata Tonkotsu Ramen Fukuoka Creamy pork-bone broth, thin straight noodles, chashu & pickled ginger — ¥700–¥900
→
Tokyo Shoyu Ramen Tokyo Clear soy broth, wavy noodles & menma — plus the world's most innovative ramen scene
→
Niigata Shoyu Ramen Niigata Crystal-clear broth with lard richness & subtle umami — Japan's most delicate ramen
→
Kyoto Tori Paitan Ramen Kyoto Creamy chicken paitan broth & rich shoyu — birthplace of Tenkaippin chain
→
Nagoya Taiwan Ramen Aichi Spicy soy broth with minced chilli meat & Chinese chives — unique to Nagoya
→

Tempura

Light batter · Seafood · Seasonal vegetables

Tempura — prawns, vegetables and seafood coated in the lightest possible batter and fried to perfection — was introduced by Portuguese merchants in the 16th century and perfected by Edo cooks into a refined art. The quality of the batter (loose, cold), sesame oil, and freshness of ingredients make all the difference.

Where to eat the best tempura in Japan

Edo-mae Tempura Tokyo Japan's oldest tempura restaurants (Daikokuya 1887) & modern omakase counters
→
Kyo-yasai Vegetable Tempura Kyoto Kyoto seasonal vegetables (shishito, myoga, yomogi) in kaiseki-style tempura courses
→
Ise Lobster & Abalone Tempura Mie Ise ebi (spiny lobster), abalone & wild akoya — served beside Ise Grand Shrine
→
Kushikatsu Osaka Shinsekai's breaded & fried skewers — the absolute rule: never double-dip
→
Mozuku & Beni-imo Tempura Okinawa Mozuku seaweed & purple sweet potato (*beni-imo*) — tempura unique to the Ryukyu islands
→
Sansai Mountain Tempura Nagano Alpine wild plants — fuki, taranome & zenmai as seasonal mountain tempura
→

Wagyu

Japanese beef · A5 marbling · Regional terroir

Wagyu (*和牛*, literally "Japanese cattle") refers to four native breeds raised under strict methods in specific regions. Intramuscular marbling (*sashi*) creates an unmatched melt-on-the-tongue texture. Three regions contest the title of Japan's finest: Kobe (Hyogo), Matsusaka (Mie), and Omi (Shiga) — the Japanese "big three."

Japan's great wagyu regions

Kobe Beef Hyogo / Kansai BMS ≥ 6, strict Tajima cattle criteria — the world's most recognised wagyu name
→
Matsusaka Beef Mie Virgin cows only, beer & massage — often cited as Japan's single finest wagyu
→
Omi Beef Shiga Japan's oldest documented wagyu tradition — raised in Shiga since the Edo period
→
Hida Beef Gifu Alpine-raised wagyu — eaten in hoba-miso (grilled magnolia leaf) style in Takayama
→
Sendai Gyutan (Beef Tongue) Miyagi Charcoal-grilled beef tongue — Sendai specialty born 1948, now a national icon
→
Kagoshima Kuroge Wagyu Kagoshima Japan's #1 wagyu producer by volume — Kuroge Washu cattle on Kyushu's grasslands
→

Street Food

Yatai · Market · B-kyū Gourmet

Japan's "B-kyū gourmet" — affordable, delicious street food — is one of the country's great food traditions. From Fukuoka's yatai stalls to Osaka's takoyaki, and Hakodate's morning market bowls, Japanese street food is clean, flavourful, and often more memorable than any starred restaurant.

Japan's best street food destinations

Takoyaki & Okonomiyaki Osaka Dotonbori & Shinsekai — the world capital of street eating, ¥500–¥1,000
→
Yatai Street Stalls Fukuoka 100+ yatai on the Nakasu riverbank — ramen, gyoza & mentaiko under the stars
→
Hiroshima Okonomiyaki Hiroshima Layered style (noodles + cabbage + egg) — different from Osaka's and fiercer about it
→
Nokkedon Bowl Aomori Furukawa Market — build your own seafood rice bowl from 20 market stalls
→
Nagoya Meshi Aichi Tebasaki wings, miso katsu, hitsumabushi & the legendary ¥400 morning set
→
Kaisen-don & Market Snacks Hokkaido Hakodate & Sapporo markets — live crabs, fresh cream cones & soup curry
→

Soba & Udon

Buckwheat · Wheat · Regional broths

Soba (buckwheat noodles) and udon (thick wheat noodles) are Japan's two great artisan noodle traditions — distinct from ramen. Soba thrives in mountain regions where buckwheat grows; udon dominates the warm plains of western Japan. Every prefecture has developed its own style, from thickness to toppings to the way they're served.

Japan's great soba & udon regions

Sanuki Udon Kagawa "Udon Prefecture" — hundreds of shops, self-service bowls from ¥200–¥400
→
Shinshu Soba Nagano Japan Alps buckwheat — Matsumoto & Nagano City's finest soba-dokoro artisan shops
→
Izumo Soba Shimane Warigo style — three stacked lacquer bowls, dark earthy soba served near Izumo Grand Shrine
→
Wanko Soba Iwate Morioka — small bowls refilled endlessly until you place the lid. Record: 500 bowls
→
Oroshi Soba Fukui Cold soba topped with freshly grated daikon — Echizen's signature dish since the Edo period
→
Inaniwa Udon Akita Silky hand-stretched flat udon for 350+ years — one of Japan's three great udon alongside Sanuki & Mizusawa
→
