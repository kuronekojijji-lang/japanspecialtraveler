Tokyo Food Guide for First Time Visitors

Last updated: March 30, 2026

What to Eat in Tokyo First Time Visitor Food Guide: The Ultimate Culinary Journey Through the World’s Greatest Food City

Meta description: This what to eat in Tokyo first time visitor food guide covers must-try dishes, best neighborhoods for eating, seasonal specialties, ordering tips, and local secrets from a 15-year resident.

Tokyo is not just a city — it’s a universe of flavor. With more Michelin stars than any other city on Earth, over 80,000 restaurants packed into its 23 wards, and a food culture that treats every dish — from a $2 bowl of standing noodles to a $500 omakase course — with genuine reverence, Tokyo is the single greatest food destination a first-time visitor could ever dream of. And that’s precisely why you need this what to eat in Tokyo first time visitor food guide before you arrive.

I’ve lived in Tokyo for over fifteen years, and I still discover new dishes, new neighborhoods, and new seasonal flavors that stop me in my tracks. The city’s culinary landscape is overwhelming in the best possible way. But for a first-time visitor, that overwhelming abundance can also be paralyzing. Where do you start? What do you order? How do you avoid tourist traps? How do you eat like a local when you can’t read the menu?

This guide is designed to answer all of those questions. I’ll walk you through the essential dishes, the best neighborhoods for eating, the seasonal specialties you shouldn’t miss, and the practical knowledge — from ticket machines to chopstick etiquette — that will transform your Tokyo food experience from good to genuinely unforgettable.

Let’s eat.

Why Tokyo’s Food Culture Is Unlike Anywhere Else

A City Built on Shokunin Spirit

To understand Tokyo’s food, you need to understand one word: shokunin (職人). It means craftsman or artisan, and it’s the philosophical foundation of Japanese cuisine. In Tokyo, it’s not unusual to find a chef who has spent 30 years perfecting a single dish — tempura, sushi, tonkatsu, yakitori. This isn’t a marketing gimmick. It’s a deeply held cultural belief that mastery comes through repetition, dedication, and an almost spiritual attention to detail.

This shokunin spirit is why a bowl of ramen at a six-seat counter in a basement in Shinjuku can be a life-changing experience. The broth may have simmered for 18 hours. The noodles may have been calibrated to a specific thickness and hydration level. The chashu pork may have been braised at a precise temperature for exactly the right number of hours. Nothing is accidental.

The Regional Melting Pot

Tokyo is also Japan’s great culinary crossroads. When people migrated to the capital from every corner of the country — Hokkaido, Kyushu, Osaka, Tohoku — they brought their regional cuisines with them. Today, you can eat Hakata-style tonkotsu ramen, Osaka-style okonomiyaki, Nagoya-style miso katsu, and Hokkaido-style soup curry all within a single Tokyo neighborhood. The city is both its own culinary tradition and a living museum of every regional food tradition in Japan.

The Seasonal Obsession

Japanese food culture is inseparable from the seasons. This isn’t just about cherry blossom-flavored Kit Kats (though those exist). It’s about shun (旬) — the concept that every ingredient has a peak moment of perfection, and eating it at that moment is one of life’s great pleasures. In Tokyo, menus change constantly. A sushi counter in March will feature different fish than the same counter in September. A kaiseki restaurant in autumn will build an entire multi-course meal around matsutake mushrooms and sanma (Pacific saury). Understanding seasonality is the key to eating extraordinarily well in Tokyo.

The Essential Dishes: What to Eat in Tokyo as a First-Time Visitor

Here are the dishes you absolutely must try on your first trip. These aren’t obscure — they’re the foundation of Tokyo’s food identity.

Sushi (寿司)

Tokyo is the birthplace of Edomae sushi — the nigiri-style sushi that the world now considers “standard.” Developed in the Edo period (1603–1868) along the shores of Tokyo Bay, Edomae sushi originally involved curing, marinating, or simmering fish to preserve it before refrigeration existed. Today, the tradition lives on in techniques like kobujime (kelp-curing) and zuke (soy-marinating tuna).

First-timer tip: Start with a kaiten-zushi (conveyor belt sushi) chain like Sushiro, Kura Sushi, or Hamazushi to get comfortable, then graduate to a counter sushi experience. Even mid-range counter sushi in Tokyo — a lunch omakase for ¥3,000–¥5,000 — will likely be the best sushi you’ve ever had.

Ramen (ラーメン)

Tokyo’s ramen scene is staggeringly diverse. The main styles you’ll encounter:

  • Shoyu (soy sauce): Tokyo’s classic. Clean, savory, often with a chicken or dashi-based broth. Light and deeply satisfying.
  • Shio (salt): Delicate and clear, highlighting the quality of the broth itself.
  • Miso: Richer and heartier, originally from Sapporo but wildly popular in Tokyo.
  • Tonkotsu: The creamy, pork-bone-based ramen from Fukuoka, found everywhere in Tokyo.
  • Tsukemen: Dipping ramen — cold noodles dunked into a concentrated, hot broth. A Tokyo invention and an absolute must-try.

Tempura (天ぷら)

Light, crispy, ethereal. Tokyo-style tempura uses a thin batter and sesame oil for frying, producing a delicate golden shell that shatters at the touch of chopsticks. Counter-style tempura restaurants, where the chef fries each piece and places it directly in front of you, are one of Tokyo’s great dining experiences.

Yakitori (焼き鳥)

Skewered, grilled chicken — but elevated to an art form. A serious yakitori restaurant will serve every part of the bird: thigh (momo), breast (mune), skin (kawa), heart (hatsu), liver (reba), cartilage (nankotsu), and the prized tail meat (bonjiri). Order omakase style (chef’s choice) at the counter and let the master guide you. Season with salt (shio) for purist flavor or tare (sweet soy glaze) for richness.

Tonkatsu (とんかつ)

A thick-cut, breaded, deep-fried pork cutlet served with shredded cabbage, rice, miso soup, and a tangy brown sauce. Tokyo has perfected this dish using heritage pork breeds like kurobuta (Berkshire) and aging techniques that produce impossibly juicy, tender cutlets. You’ll be given a small mortar with sesame seeds to grind yourself — this freshly ground sesame mixed with the sauce is pure magic.

Soba (そば)

Buckwheat noodles, served cold with a dipping sauce (zaru soba) or hot in a broth (kake soba). Tokyo has a centuries-old soba tradition, and the best shops make their noodles fresh daily — sometimes right in front of you. Cold soba in summer and hot soba in winter is a Tokyo ritual. Don’t miss toshikoshi soba if you’re visiting on New Year’s Eve — eating these “year-crossing noodles” is one of Japan’s most beloved traditions.

Unagi (うなぎ)

Freshwater eel, glazed with a sweet soy-based tare sauce and grilled over charcoal. Served over rice as unadon or in a lacquered box as unaju, this is one of Tokyo’s most luxurious comfort foods. Traditionally eaten during the hottest days of summer (the midsummer Day of the Ox, typically in late July) to boost stamina, but available and delicious year-round.

Monjayaki (もんじゃ焼き)

This is Tokyo’s own version of the savory pancake — messier, runnier, and more fun than Osaka’s okonomiyaki. You mix ingredients on a hot griddle, spread the batter, and scrape it up with small metal spatulas called hera as it crisps at the edges. It’s a social, interactive meal and a uniquely Tokyo experience. The spiritual home of monjayaki is the Tsukishima neighborhood.

Best Neighborhoods to Eat In: Where to Find Tokyo’s Greatest Food

Rather than listing specific restaurants — which open, close, and change quality constantly — here are the neighborhoods where you’ll consistently find outstanding food. Walk these streets, follow your nose, and trust the lines.

Shinjuku (新宿)

Shinjuku is sensory overload in the best way. Head to Omoide Yokocho (Memory Lane, also called “Piss Alley” — don’t let the name deter you) for yakitori and beer at tiny, smoke-filled stalls that have been serving since the post-war era. The area around Shinjuku Station’s east and west exits is dense with ramen shops, izakaya (Japanese pubs), and department store basement food halls (depachika) that are destinations in themselves. For late-night eating, Kabukicho’s side streets deliver everything from tsukemen to Korean-style yakiniku.

Tsukiji Outer Market (築地場外市場)

The famous inner fish market moved to Toyosu in 2018, but the outer market at Tsukiji remains one of Tokyo’s greatest food destinations. Dozens of stalls and small restaurants serve sushi, tamagoyaki (sweet Japanese omelet), fresh seafood bowls, and grilled scallops. Go early — by 7:00 or 8:00 AM — to beat the worst crowds. Weekday mornings are significantly less hectic than weekends.

Yanaka / Nezu / Sendagi (谷根千)

This charming, old-Tokyo neighborhood — collectively nicknamed “Yanesen” — is where you’ll find traditional shitamachi (downtown) food culture. Small senbei (rice cracker) shops where crackers are grilled to order, centuries-old wagashi (Japanese sweets) makers, hole-in-the-wall tonkatsu shops, and cozy kissaten (retro coffee shops) serving hand-dripped coffee and thick-cut toast. Yanaka Ginza shopping street is a food walk you’ll never forget.

Ebisu / Daikanyama (恵比寿・代官山)

A slightly more refined neighborhood with excellent izakaya, wine bars, and modern Japanese restaurants. Ebisu’s Yokocho (a retro-style indoor alley of small restaurants) is lively and welcoming to foreigners. The area around Ebisu Station’s west exit is dense with quality dining at every price point. This is where many Tokyo chefs eat on their nights off — always a good sign.

Koenji / Asagaya (高円寺・阿佐ヶ谷)

If you want to eat like a young, broke, food-obsessed Tokyoite, these neighboring stations on the Chuo Line are your answer. Cheap and phenomenal ramen, curry rice shops, standing bars (tachinomi) where beers start at ¥200, and a wonderfully bohemian atmosphere. Asagaya’s Pearl Center shotengai (covered shopping street) is full of hidden gems.

Asakusa (浅草)

Tokyo’s oldest entertainment district is the place for heritage food experiences. Tempura restaurants that have been operating since the Edo period, unagi shops with century-old tare sauce, and handmade soba served in tatami rooms. The area around Senso-ji temple is touristy, but walk five minutes in any direction and you’ll find deeply authentic, multi-generational restaurants.

Tsukishima (月島)

The undisputed capital of monjayaki. Monja Street (Monja-dori) has over 70 restaurants specializing in this uniquely Tokyo dish. It’s fun, it’s cheap, and it’s a perfect activity for groups. Combine it with a walk along the Sumida River waterfront and you have a perfect Tokyo afternoon.

Best Time to Visit for Seasonal Food: A Month-by-Month What to Eat in Tokyo First Time Visitor Food Guide

Tokyo rewards every season with unique flavors. Here’s what to seek out month by month:

January–February (Winter)

  • Oden: Simmered fish cakes, daikon radish, and eggs in a fragrant dashi broth. Available at convenience stores (honestly excellent) and specialty restaurants.
  • Fugu (blowfish): Winter is peak fugu season. Try it as sashimi (tessa), hot pot (tecchiri), or deep-fried (fugu karaage).
  • Nabe (hot pot): Every izakaya offers some form of communal hot pot in winter. Chanko nabe (sumo wrestler’s stew) is a Tokyo classic.
  • Strawberries: Japanese strawberries peak in January–March and are absurdly sweet and perfect.

March–April (Spring)

  • Sakura-themed everything: Cherry blossom mochi, sakura-flavored drinks, and hanami (flower viewing) bento boxes.
  • Tai (sea bream): Spring is peak season. Look for it as sashimi or grilled at sushi counters.
  • Takenoko (bamboo shoots): Freshly dug bamboo shoots appear in tempura, rice dishes, and nimono (simmered dishes) throughout April.
  • Hanami food culture: Pack a bento, grab some drinks from a conbini, and eat under the cherry blossoms in Ueno Park, Shinjuku Gyoen (no alcohol allowed), or along the Meguro River. Cherry blossoms typically peak around March 25–April 5 in Tokyo.

May–June (Early Summer)

  • Katsuo (bonito): The first catch of the season — hatsu-gatsuo — is a celebrated spring-to-summer delicacy. Seared on the outside, raw inside, served with ginger and garlic. Exquisite.
  • Edamame: Fresh, in-season edamame at an izakaya in June is a completely different food from frozen edamame elsewhere in the world.
  • Ume (plum) season: Umeshu (plum wine) is prepared in June. Many restaurants serve ume-flavored dishes and drinks.

July–August (Summer)

  • Unagi: Midsummer is traditionally unagi season. The Day of the Ox (Doyo no Ushi no Hi) falls in late July, and queues at unagi restaurants are enormous — but worth it.
  • Kakigori: Shaved ice with syrups, condensed milk, or fruit. Tokyo’s artisan kakigori scene has exploded in recent years, with some shops using natural ice harvested from mountain lakes.
  • Cold soba and cold ramen (hiyashi chuka): Refreshing noodle dishes perfect for Tokyo’s brutally humid summers.
  • Beer gardens: Rooftop beer gardens open across the city from May through September. Department store rooftops are classic locations.

September–October (Autumn)

  • Sanma (Pacific saury): The quintessential autumn fish. Grilled whole with a squeeze of sudachi citrus and a mound of grated daikon. Peak season is late September through October.
  • Matsutake mushrooms: Wildly expensive, intensely aromatic. Even a small dish of matsutake gohan (rice cooked with matsutake) is worth the splurge.
  • Kuri (chestnuts): Chestnut rice, chestnut sweets, Mont Blanc pastries — autumn in Tokyo is chestnut paradise.
  • Sweet potatoes (satsumaimo): Roasted sweet potato vendors appear on street corners. The smell is autumn in Tokyo.

November–December (Late Autumn / Early Winter)

  • Oysters: Hiroshima oysters are in full season. Oyster bars and izakaya feature them raw, grilled, and fried.
  • Yuzu: This fragrant citrus appears in everything from soba broth to cocktails to hot baths. December is peak yuzu season.
  • Osechi ryori (New Year cuisine): If you’re visiting over New Year’s, seek out osechi — the elaborately arranged boxes of symbolic dishes eaten in the first days of January. Many hotels and department stores sell beautiful osechi sets.
  • Christmas cake: Japan’s quirky Christmas tradition of eating strawberry shortcake on December 24 is a cultural experience worth tasting.

How to Order and Eat Like a Local: Practical Tips for First-Timers

The Ticket Machine (券売機 / Kenbaiki)

Many ramen shops, gyudon (beef bowl) chains, and budget restaurants use a vending machine at the entrance instead of a waiter. Here’s the process:

  1. Look for photos or plastic food models outside to decide what you want before entering.
  2. Insert cash (some newer machines accept IC cards or credit cards).
  3. Press the button for your dish. Top-left button is almost always the house specialty or most popular item — a safe bet if you can’t read the menu.
  4. Take your ticket(s) and hand them to the staff. Find a seat.
  5. Eat. Leave. No tipping.

Chopstick Basics

  • Never stick chopsticks vertically into a bowl of rice — this resembles funeral incense offerings.
  • Don’t pass food chopstick-to-chopstick — this mirrors a funeral rite.
  • It’s perfectly fine to lift bowls of rice and soup to your mouth. In fact, it’s expected.
  • Slurping noodles is not just acceptable — it’s encouraged. It aerates the noodles and shows appreciation.

Saying the Right Things

  • Itadakimasu (いただきます): Say this before eating. It means “I humbly receive” and is a universal expression of gratitude.
  • Gochisousama deshita (ごちそうさまでした): Say this when you finish eating or when leaving the restaurant. It means “Thank you for the meal.”
  • Sumimasen (すみません): “Excuse me” — use this to get a server’s attention. Much more effective than trying to make eye contact.
  • Okanjo onegaishimasu (お勘定お願いします): “Check, please.”

Navigating Menus

Many restaurants — especially in tourist-heavy areas — now have English menus or picture menus. If not:

  • Look for plastic food displays (sampuru) in the window. You can point at what you want or take a photo and show the staff.
  • Google Translate’s camera feature works reasonably well on Japanese menus in a pinch.
  • The phrase “osusume wa nan desu ka?" (おすすめは何ですか？) means “What do you recommend?” and is your secret weapon.

Izakaya Ordering

Izakaya (居酒屋) are Japanese gastropubs and arguably the best way to experience Tokyo’s food breadth in one sitting. Here’s how they work:

  1. You’ll often receive an otoshi (お通し) — a small appetizer that appears without ordering. This is normal and functions as a cover charge (usually ¥300–¥500). It’s not optional.
  2. Order drinks first, then food. Beer (nama biiru / 生ビール) is the classic first order.
  3. Order a few dishes at a time and share family-style. Don’t order everything at once.
  4. Typical progression: edamame → sashimi → grilled items → fried items → rice or noodles to finish.

Price Guide: How Much Does Food Cost in Tokyo?

One of Tokyo’s great surprises is how affordable it can be. You can eat extraordinarily well on a backpacker’s budget.

Budget (¥500–¥1,500 per meal / ~$3.50–$10)

  • Convenience stores (conbini): Don’t laugh. 7-Eleven, Lawson, and FamilyMart sell onigiri (rice balls), sandwiches, fried chicken, oden, and bento boxes that are genuinely delicious. A filling conbini meal costs ¥500–¥800.
  • Gyudon chains: Yoshinoya, Matsuya, and Sukiya serve beef bowls for ¥400–¥600. Fast, filling, and honest.
  • Standing soba/udon shops: Found in and around train stations. A bowl of hot soba for ¥300–¥500. The ultimate quick meal.
  • Ramen: A bowl at most shops costs ¥800–¥1,200. Add toppings for ¥100–¥300 each.

Mid-Range (¥1,500–¥5,000 per meal / ~$10–$35)

  • Lunch sets (teishoku): Many restaurants that charge ¥8,000+ at dinner offer lunch sets for ¥1,500–¥3,000. This is the single best hack for eating high-quality food in Tokyo on a budget. Sushi, tempura, tonkatsu, and kaiseki restaurants all do this.
  • Izakaya: A full izakaya experience with drinks and shared plates typically runs ¥3,000–¥5,000 per person.
  • Kaiten-zushi: Conveyor belt sushi averages ¥1,000–¥2,500 per person depending on how much you eat.
  • Depachika (department store food halls): Gorgeous takeaway bento, sushi, wagashi, and prepared foods. Budget ¥1,000–¥2,000 for a beautiful meal.

Splurge (¥10,000–¥50,000+ per meal / ~$70–$350+)

  • Omakase sushi: Counter sushi with the chef selecting pieces for you. Lunch courses start around ¥5,000–¥10,000 at many excellent restaurants. Dinner omakase at prestigious establishments runs ¥20,000–¥50,000+.
  • Kaiseki (multi-course Japanese haute cuisine): ¥15,000–¥40,000. A kaiseki dinner is not just a meal — it’s an art exhibition you eat.
  • High-end tempura or unagi: Counter-style experiences run ¥8,000–¥20,000.
  • Wagyu beef: Teppanyaki or yakiniku featuring A5 wagyu runs ¥10,000–¥30,000+ per person.

Local tip: Tokyo’s quality-to-price ratio is unmatched globally. A ¥1,000 ramen in Tokyo would cost $25 in New York and be half as good. Don’t feel like you need to spend big to eat well here.

Nearby Sights to Combine with Your Food Trip

Tokyo’s food neighborhoods double as sightseeing destinations. Here are natural combinations:

Tsukiji Outer Market + Hama-rikyu Gardens + Ginza

Start with an early-morning seafood feast at Tsukiji, walk to the serene Hama-rikyu Gardens (¥300 entry; enjoy matcha and wagashi at the teahouse on the pond), then stroll through Ginza’s elegant streets for afternoon shopping and people-watching. The depachika at Ginza Mitsukoshi or Ginza Six are spectacular for an afternoon food browse.

Asakusa + Kappabashi + Ueno

Begin with tempura or soba near Senso-ji, walk to Kappabashi Kitchen Street (Tokyo’s restaurant supply district — pick up Japanese knives, ceramics, and those incredible plastic food samples as souvenirs), then continue to Ueno for the parks, museums, and Ameyoko market’s chaotic, wonderful energy.

Tsukishima Monjayaki + Toyosu Market + teamLab Planets

Make monjayaki for a late breakfast on Monja Street, visit the Toyosu Fish Market’s observation areas (free; tuna auction viewing requires advance reservation from about 5:30 AM), then walk to teamLab Planets for the immersive art experience.

Shinjuku Food Crawl + Golden Gai + Tokyo Metropolitan Government Building

Eat your way through Omoide Yokocho at dusk, bar-hop through the miniature bars of Golden Gai (most bars seat 6-8 people; check for cover charges at the door), then head to the Tokyo Metropolitan Government Building’s free observation deck for night views of the city you’ve just been devouring.

Yanaka Food Walk + Nezu Shrine + Ueno Park

Graze your way down Yanaka Ginza (try the menchi katsu — fried meat croquettes — from the shop with the longest line), visit the stunning Nezu Shrine (especially breathtaking during the azalea festival in mid-April to early May), then wander through Ueno Park.

Getting There and Around

Getting to Tokyo

  • Narita International Airport (NRT): About 60–90 minutes from central Tokyo. The Narita Express (N’EX) to Tokyo/Shinjuku/Shibuya stations costs ¥3,070 (one-way) and is the most comfortable option. The Keisei Skyliner to Ueno/Nippori is ¥2,520 and slightly faster. Budget option: Access Express to Asakusa/Nihombashi for ¥1,270.
  • Haneda International Airport (HND): Much closer to central Tokyo — 15–45 minutes. The Tokyo Monorail to Hamamatsucho (¥500) or Keikyu Line to Shinagawa/central Tokyo (¥300–¥500) are fast and cheap.

Getting Around for Food

  • Get a Suica or Pasmo IC card immediately upon arrival (available at airport station ticket machines or via the Suica app on iPhone). Tap and ride on all trains, subways, and buses. Can also be used at convenience stores and many vending machines.
  • The subway is your best friend. Tokyo Metro and Toei Subway lines cover virtually every food neighborhood in this guide. A Tokyo Subway 24/48/72-hour ticket (¥800/¥1,200/¥1,500) offers unlimited rides and is exceptional value if you plan to neighborhood-hop.
  • Walk. Tokyo’s food neighborhoods reward wandering. Some of the best discoveries happen when you turn down the alley you weren’t planning to explore.
  • Avoid taxis for routine transport — they’re expensive (¥500+ base fare) and Tokyo traffic can be brutal. Save them for late-night returns when trains stop (last trains run around midnight–12:30 AM; first trains resume around 5:00 AM).

Critical local tip: Many of Tokyo’s best restaurants are in basements and upper floors of nondescript buildings. If you’re looking for a specific place and can’t find it at street level, check the building directory panel near the elevator. Some of the city’s greatest meals are hiding on the 4th floor of a building you’d otherwise walk past.

Where to Stay for the Best Food Access

The best neighborhoods to base yourself for a food-focused Tokyo trip:

  • Shinjuku: Central, endlessly vibrant, and surrounded by food at all hours. Excellent transport hub.
  • Shibuya / Ebisu: Trendy, walkable, with outstanding dining density and easy access to everywhere.
  • Asakusa: Traditional atmosphere, great breakfast options at Tsukiji (one stop away), and a quieter nighttime experience.
  • Ginza / Nihonbashi: Upscale area with high-end dining and easy access to Tsukiji.

Booking tip: Reserve your accommodation through a platform that offers free cancellation, and book early — especially during cherry blossom season (late March–early April), Golden Week (April 29–May 5), and autumn foliage season (mid-November to early December). Hotels in food-rich neighborhoods sell out fast during peak periods.

Local Tips: Things Only Tokyo Residents Know

  1. Lunch is the golden hour. The exact same chef cooking the exact same food at dinner often serves a lunch course for 30–50% of the price. Sushi, tempura, kaiseki — always check the lunch menu first.

  2. Department store basement floors (depachika) start discounting prepared foods around 30–60 minutes before closing. You’ll see yellow discount stickers (usually 20–50% off) on sushi, bento, and prepared dishes. This is how many frugal Tokyoites eat gourmet food on a budget.

  3. The best ramen shops often have the worst-looking exteriors. A hand-written sign, a steamed-up window, and a line of salarymen at 11:45 AM is your quality indicator.

  4. Tabelog (tabelog.com) is Japan’s real restaurant rating site — far more trusted than Google reviews or TripAdvisor among locals. A Tabelog score above 3.5 is genuinely excellent. Above 4.0 is exceptional. The site is in Japanese, but Google Translate handles it reasonably well.

  5. Many high-end restaurants require reservations weeks or months in advance and only accept bookings in Japanese by phone. If your hotel has a concierge, use them. Some booking services (like Tableall, Omakase, or Pocket Concierge) offer English-language reservations for a fee — often worth it for special-occasion meals.

  6. Convenience store food is not a compromise — it’s a category. 7-Eleven’s onigiri, Lawson’s karaage-kun fried chicken, and FamilyMart’s famichiki are legitimately beloved by Japanese people. A late-night conbini run is a genuine Tokyo food experience.

  7. Tap water in Tokyo is safe and excellent. Don’t waste money on bottled water. Many ramen shops and restaurants serve water automatically — just look for the self-service water pitcher or dispenser.

  8. Cash is still king in many small restaurants. While Japan is rapidly adopting cashless payment, plenty of the best tiny ramen shops, yakitori joints, and soba restaurants are cash only. Carry at least ¥5,000–¥10,000 in cash at all times. 7-Eleven ATMs reliably accept international cards.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Tokyo good for vegetarian or vegan visitors?

Tokyo has improved dramatically, but it remains challenging compared to Western cities. Dashi (fish-based stock) is in almost everything, including many dishes that appear vegetarian. Your best bets are shojin ryori (Buddhist temple cuisine — entirely plant-based), Indian/Nepalese restaurants (abundant in Tokyo), and the growing number of dedicated vegan restaurants in Shibuya, Shimokitazawa, and Daikanyama. The app HappyCow is invaluable for finding options. Learn the phrase “watashi wa niku to sakana wo tabemasen” (I don’t eat meat or fish) — though be aware that dashi may still be present in broths and sauces unless you specifically ask.

Do I need to tip in restaurants?

Absolutely not. Tipping does not exist in Japanese food culture and can actually cause confusion or embarrassment. The price on the menu is what you pay (plus tax, which is usually included). Excellent service is considered a baseline standard, not something that requires extra monetary reward.

How do I handle food allergies?

Bring an allergy card written in Japanese that lists your allergies. Several free templates are available online (search “Japanese allergy card”). Allergies to shellfish, wheat, soy, and buckwheat (soba) should be communicated clearly, as these are foundational ingredients in Japanese cooking. Most restaurant staff will take allergy cards seriously and do their best to accommodate you.

Is it rude to leave food on my plate?

It’s considered good manners to finish everything, especially rice. If you’re served more than you can eat, it’s better to mention at the time of ordering that you’d like a smaller portion (“sukuname de onegaishimasu” — less, please). At ramen shops, you can usually specify noodle quantity when ordering.

What time do restaurants open and close?

Lunch service typically runs 11:00 AM–2:00 PM (last order around 1:30 PM). Many restaurants close for a break from 2:00–5:00 PM. Dinner service usually runs 5:00/6:00 PM–10:00/11:00 PM. Ramen shops, izakaya, and places near entertainment districts may stay open later. Very few good restaurants are open continuously from lunch through dinner — plan accordingly and don’t show up at 3:00 PM expecting a full meal at most sit-down restaurants.
