Best Ramen in Sapporo: A Local's Guide

Last updated: March 30, 2026

Best Ramen in Sapporo Locals Guide: The Ultimate Winter Pilgrimage for Noodle Lovers

Meta description: A locals-approved guide to the best ramen in Sapporo covering iconic miso ramen districts, secret ordering tips, seasonal highlights, and winter travel advice for Hokkaido’s noodle capital.

If you’ve ever slurped a bowl of ramen on a freezing January night in Sapporo — steam rising from a rich, amber-colored miso broth, your glasses fogged beyond use, your fingers slowly thawing around the ceramic bowl — you understand something that no food photo can capture. This is the best ramen in Sapporo locals guide you’ll ever need, written by someone who has spent fifteen winters making the pilgrimage north to Hokkaido specifically for this experience.

Sapporo isn’t just another ramen city. It’s the ramen city — the birthplace of miso ramen, a style so deeply tied to this place that eating it anywhere else feels like a photocopy of the original. And winter is when Sapporo ramen reaches its spiritual peak. When temperatures plunge to minus 10°C and the snow piles waist-high along Tanuki-koji, that first spoonful of broth doesn’t just taste good. It feels like survival. It feels like love.

I’ve lived in Japan for fifteen years, and I’ve eaten ramen in every prefecture. But I keep coming back to Sapporo in winter. This guide will tell you exactly why — and exactly how to experience it for yourself.

Why Sapporo Is the Undisputed Miso Ramen Capital of Japan

A Brief History of Sapporo Ramen

To understand Sapporo ramen, you need to understand the city itself. Sapporo is young by Japanese standards — it wasn’t formally established until 1868, when the Meiji government began developing Hokkaido as Japan’s northern frontier. The city grew quickly, populated by settlers from across Japan who brought diverse food traditions. This melting-pot energy created a culinary openness that other, more tradition-bound cities lacked.

Ramen itself arrived in Sapporo in the early 20th century, initially as a Chinese-influenced noodle soup served in simple eateries near Susukino, the city’s entertainment district. These early bowls were shoyu (soy sauce) based, similar to what you’d find in Tokyo or Hakodate. But in 1955, everything changed.

The widely credited origin story centers on a cook named Morito Omiya at a shop called Aji no Sanpei in the Minami 1-jo area. According to local lore, Omiya began experimenting with miso as a ramen base — an unconventional choice at the time, since miso was associated with home-style cooking, not restaurant noodles. His miso ramen featured a thick pork-and-chicken broth, stir-fried vegetables (particularly bean sprouts, cabbage, and onions), curly yellow noodles, and a generous scoop of miso paste dissolved into the soup. The result was heavier, richer, and more calorie-dense than other ramen styles — perfectly suited for Hokkaido’s brutal winters.

By the 1960s, miso ramen had become synonymous with Sapporo. The famous “Ramen Yokocho” (Ramen Alley) was already drawing visitors. When the 1972 Winter Olympics brought international attention to the city, Sapporo miso ramen entered the global consciousness for the first time.

What Makes Sapporo Miso Ramen Authentic

Not all miso ramen is Sapporo-style. Here’s what defines the real thing:

The broth: A blend of pork bone (tonkotsu) and chicken stock, simmered for hours until rich and opaque. This base is heavier than the delicate chintan broths found in Tokyo. Many shops also incorporate dried fish (niboshi) or kombu for umami depth.

The miso: Sapporo shops typically use a blend of red miso (aka-miso) and white miso (shiro-miso), often a proprietary ratio that differs from shop to shop. Some places age their miso, some blend three or four varieties. The miso is usually dissolved into the broth by being stir-fried in a hot wok with lard or sesame oil — this step, called miso wo yaku (焼く味噌, “grilling the miso”), caramelizes the paste slightly and adds a toasty, complex flavor you won’t find in miso ramen elsewhere.

The noodles: Thick, curly, and yellow, with a high moisture content and chewy texture. These noodles are egg-based and specifically designed to trap the heavy broth in their waves and curls. The standard firmness is softer than what you’d find in Hakata-style tonkotsu — they’re meant to absorb, not resist.

The toppings: Stir-fried vegetables are non-negotiable. Moyashi (bean sprouts), thinly sliced onion, and cabbage are the holy trinity. Chashu (braised pork slices), menma (fermented bamboo shoots), corn kernels, and a knob of butter are common additions. The corn-and-butter combination is distinctly Hokkaido — a nod to the island’s agricultural identity as Japan’s dairy and farming heartland.

The lard: A thin layer of melted lard floats on the surface of authentic Sapporo miso ramen. This isn’t an accident or a shortcut — it’s engineering. The lard acts as an insulating layer, keeping the broth piping hot far longer than it would otherwise stay warm. In a city where winter temperatures regularly drop below minus 10°C, this detail matters enormously. Your ramen stays hot to the last slurp.

The Three Major Ramen Styles of Hokkaido

While this guide focuses on Sapporo’s miso ramen, it’s worth knowing that Hokkaido actually has three distinct regional ramen traditions:

  • Sapporo: Miso-based, rich, with stir-fried vegetables and curly noodles
  • Asahikawa: Shoyu (soy sauce) based, with a double-stock (pork bone + seafood) broth and a signature lard layer. The noodles are thinner and the overall flavor is more intense.
  • Hakodate: Shio (salt) based, with a clear, delicate broth and straight noodles. This is the lightest and most elegant of Hokkaido’s three styles.

If you have time during your Hokkaido winter trip, tasting all three creates a beautiful contrast. But if you only have one city to visit, Sapporo is the right choice. The density of excellent shops, the walkability of the ramen districts, and the sheer cultural weight of the miso ramen tradition here make it unbeatable.

Best Ramen in Sapporo: Where Locals Actually Eat (District by District)

Rather than naming specific shops — which open and close, change hours, and shift quality over time — I’ll point you to the districts and areas where Sapporo’s ramen culture is most concentrated and vibrant. These neighborhoods have sustained excellent ramen for decades and are where locals instinctively head when the craving hits.

1. Ganso Sapporo Ramen Yokocho (Original Ramen Alley) — Minami 5-jo

This narrow, neon-lit alley in the Susukino entertainment district is where Sapporo’s ramen legend began. Established in 1951, Ramen Yokocho packs roughly 17 tiny shops into an alley you can walk in under a minute. Each shop seats between 8 and 15 people at a counter — no tables, no frills.

Why locals go: Despite its fame, several shops here are still genuinely excellent. The key is to go on weekday nights (Tuesday through Thursday) when tourists thin out. Locals tend to have “their” shop in the alley and will return to the same stool for years. The atmosphere — crammed shoulder-to-shoulder, slurping in a steam-filled room barely bigger than your hotel bathroom — is irreplaceable.

Best time to visit: 9:00–10:30 PM on weeknights. The after-work crowd has cleared, but kitchens are still in full rhythm.

2. Shin (New) Ramen Yokocho — Minami 4-jo

Located just one block north of the original alley, Shin Ramen Yokocho opened later to accommodate the overflow of ramen shops that couldn’t fit in the original. It has a similar narrow-alley format but with slightly newer shops and, often, shorter lines.

Why locals go: Several innovative shops here push beyond classic miso — you’ll find excellent shoyu and shio options alongside miso bowls with creative twists (spicy miso, seafood miso, etc.). If you want to compare styles within walking distance, start at Shin Yokocho before heading to the original.

3. Susukino Station Area — Minami 3-jo to Minami 6-jo

The broader Susukino district, beyond the two yokocho alleys, is saturated with standalone ramen shops. Walking along Minami 4-jo-dori or the streets just south of Susukino Station, you’ll pass dozens of ramen-ya, many of which are local institutions with 30+ year track records.

Why locals go: This is where you find the “hidden champion” shops — places with no English signage, no TripAdvisor presence, just a line of salary workers at 11:30 PM waiting in the snow. Look for shops with hand-written menus, a single counter, and a master who works alone or with one assistant. These micro-operations are often the most rewarding.

Local tip: The late-night ramen culture in Susukino is real. Many shops open until 2:00 or 3:00 AM. A bowl of miso ramen after a night of drinking (a ritual called shime no ramen, “closing ramen”) is a cornerstone of Sapporo nightlife.

4. Sapporo Station (Kita-guchi / North Exit Area)

The area around JR Sapporo Station’s north exit has developed a thriving ramen scene in recent years, partly driven by the redevelopment of the Kita 6-jo and Kita 8-jo areas. Several well-known shops have opened branches here, alongside newer operations by young chefs.

Why locals go: Convenience. If you’re arriving by train from the airport or other Hokkaido cities, you can get a world-class bowl within a five-minute walk of the station without even taking the subway. The competition in this area has driven quality very high.

5. Sumikawa / Nango Area — Southern Suburbs

This residential zone south of the city center, accessible via the Nanboku subway line, is where many of Sapporo’s most dedicated ramen artisans choose to open shop. Without the foot traffic of Susukino, these shops survive purely on the quality of their bowls and the loyalty of local regulars.

Why locals go: If you want to eat where actual Sapporo residents eat — not tourists, not business travelers, not Instagram hunters — take the subway south. The experience of walking through a quiet, snow-covered residential neighborhood and ducking into a tiny shop where everyone at the counter is a regular is something you’ll remember for years.

Getting there: Take the Nanboku Line to Sumikawa Station or Nango 7-chome Station and walk.

6. Tanukikoji Shopping Arcade and Odori Area

The covered Tanukikoji arcade stretches for nearly a kilometer through central Sapporo, and the blocks surrounding Odori Park host numerous ramen shops that range from classic institutions to modern interpretations. The advantage here is that the arcade is covered, making it perfect for exploring on particularly harsh winter days.

Why locals go: Lunchtime ramen. The Odori area is dense with office buildings, and local workers flood these shops between 11:30 AM and 1:00 PM. Quality has to be consistent because the lunch crowd is brutally discerning — serve one bad bowl and they won’t come back.

7. Sapporo Ramen Republic (Sapporo Esta / Stellar Place Area)

Located in the upper floors of commercial buildings near Sapporo Station, these curated ramen floors feature rotating selections of shops from across Hokkaido. While the concept is somewhat tourist-oriented, the shops that earn a spot here are legitimately excellent — they’ve been vetted by the building operators and face fierce competition for their lease.

Why locals go: Variety. If you want to taste Asahikawa-style shoyu or Hakodate-style shio alongside Sapporo miso in a single visit, these curated floors make it easy. They’re also climate-controlled, which matters when it’s minus 15°C outside.

Best Time to Visit Sapporo for Ramen: A Seasonal Breakdown

While you can eat outstanding ramen in Sapporo year-round, the experience varies dramatically by season. Here’s an honest month-by-month assessment:

Month Temperature Ramen Experience Notes
January -7°C to -1°C ★★★★★ Peak winter. Snow festival late month. Ramen at its most essential.
February -7°C to -1°C ★★★★★ Sapporo Snow Festival (early Feb). Cold intensifies the broth experience. Crowds at festival, but ramen shops outside Odori stay manageable.
March -3°C to 4°C ★★★★ Still cold. Snow melting. Fewer tourists. Excellent ramen month.
April 3°C to 12°C ★★★ Spring arrives late. Cherry blossoms in Sapporo around May 1-5 (much later than Honshu). Ramen still hits right.
May 9°C to 18°C ★★★ Pleasant. Lilac Festival. Ramen satisfying but you may prefer lighter dishes.
June 13°C to 21°C ★★★ Hokkaido has no rainy season (unlike Honshu). Comfortable. Hiyashi (cold) ramen starts appearing.
July 17°C to 25°C ★★ Summer. Hot ramen feels heavy. Some shops offer limited summer menus.
August 18°C to 26°C ★★ Warmest month. Consider cold noodle alternatives like hiyashi chuka.
September 14°C to 22°C ★★★ Autumn begins. Ramen cravings return. Fall food festivals.
October 7°C to 16°C ★★★★ Autumn foliage. Temperature drops make miso ramen deeply satisfying again.
November 1°C to 8°C ★★★★★ First snows. Winter ramen season begins in earnest.
December -4°C to 2°C ★★★★★ Full winter. Holiday illuminations. Ramen is medicine.

The sweet spot: Late January through February. You get the Sapporo Snow Festival, the most extreme cold (which makes every bowl transcendent), and access to the best seasonal Hokkaido ingredients. Crab, scallops, and uni from Hokkaido’s coast often appear in special ramen creations at this time.

Seasonal specials to watch for:

  • Winter (Dec–Feb): Butter-corn miso ramen is at its most popular. Some shops create limited-edition bowls featuring Hokkaido crab or sea urchin.
  • Spring (Mar–May): Fresh spring onions and garlic shoots appear as seasonal toppings.
  • Summer (Jun–Aug): Cold ramen (hiyashi ramen) and tsukemen (dipping noodles) dominate menus.
  • Autumn (Sep–Nov): Rich, heavy miso bowls return. Some shops add roasted fall vegetables.

How to Order and Eat Ramen in Sapporo: A First-Timer’s Guide

The Ordering Process

Most ramen shops in Sapporo use one of two ordering systems:

Ticket machine (kenbaiki 券売機): You’ll encounter a vending machine near the entrance. Insert cash (¥1,000 bills and coins — many machines don’t accept ¥5,000 or ¥10,000 bills, and credit cards are rare), press the button for your selection, and hand the ticket to the staff when you sit down. Newer machines at popular shops sometimes have English labels; most don’t.

Verbal ordering: Smaller, traditional shops skip the machine. Just sit at the counter, and the master will ask what you’d like. “Miso ramen” is universally understood. Point at photos on the wall if available.

Customization Options

At many Sapporo shops, you can customize:

  • Noodle firmness: Katame (firm), futsuu (normal), yawarakame (soft). Unlike Hakata ramen where firm is king, Sapporo locals generally prefer futsuu or slightly soft.
  • Broth richness: Kotteri (rich/heavy) or assari (light). For your first Sapporo miso ramen, go kotteri — it’s the authentic experience.
  • Garlic: Some shops ask if you want ninniku (garlic). Say hai (yes). It belongs in miso ramen.
  • Spice level: If ordering kara-miso, you may be asked to choose a spice level from 1 to 5. Start at 2 — Hokkaido “spicy” is real.

Eating Etiquette

Slurp loudly. This is not a suggestion; it’s practically a requirement. Slurping aerates the noodles, cools them, and enhances the flavor. In Sapporo’s tiny ramen shops, the sound of six people slurping simultaneously is the ambient music.

Eat quickly. Ramen is not a leisurely meal. The noodles absorb broth and go soft within minutes. Aim to finish within 10-15 minutes of your bowl arriving.

Drink the broth. You don’t have to finish it, but tasting the broth on its own — not just as a vehicle for noodles — is how you appreciate the master’s craft. Lift the bowl with both hands and sip directly.

Don’t ask for modifications to the recipe. In Japan, ramen is the chef’s art. You can request standard customizations (firmness, size), but asking to substitute ingredients or make unusual changes is considered disrespectful. Eat the bowl as the master intended it.

Price Guide: What to Expect to Pay

Ramen in Sapporo remains one of Japan’s best food values. Here’s the current landscape:

Budget (¥700–¥900)

A basic miso ramen at a no-frills shop. This gets you the standard bowl with broth, noodles, stir-fried vegetables, one or two slices of chashu, and menma. No extras. This is what most local workers eat for lunch, and the quality is often exceptional — price does not necessarily correlate with quality in Sapporo’s ramen world.

Mid-Range (¥900–¥1,200)

The standard experience at most well-known shops. This buys you a topping-enhanced bowl — extra chashu, ajitama (seasoned egg), butter and corn, perhaps nori. Many visitors land in this range naturally.

Splurge (¥1,200–¥1,800)

Premium bowls featuring luxury Hokkaido ingredients: crab, sea urchin, thick-cut chashu from heritage pork breeds, or limited-edition seasonal specials. Some shops offer “all-topping” bowls (zenbu nose) in this range. Adding a side of gyoza (¥350–¥500) and rice (¥150–¥250) pushes the total meal cost here.

Important note on payment: Many ramen shops in Sapporo are cash only. This is changing slowly, but as of recent years, approximately half of the small independent shops do not accept credit cards or even IC cards. Always carry at least ¥2,000–¥3,000 in cash when ramen hunting. There are 7-Eleven ATMs (which accept international cards) on virtually every major block in central Sapporo.

Tipping: Does not exist in Japan. Do not leave money on the counter.

Nearby Sights to Combine with Your Sapporo Ramen Trip

A winter trip to Sapporo for ramen should include these experiences, all of which pair naturally with your noodle pilgrimage:

Sapporo Snow Festival (Sapporo Yuki Matsuri) — Early February

Japan’s most famous winter festival features enormous ice and snow sculptures along Odori Park, a community sculpture site in Tsudome, and illuminated ice sculptures in Susukino. The Susukino venue is literally steps from Ramen Yokocho — you can admire ice sculptures and then duck into the alley for a steaming bowl. Dates are typically the first full week of February (check official dates, as they shift slightly each year). Free entry.

Nijo Market (Nijo Ichiba) — Year-round, Best in Winter

Sapporo’s central seafood market, a 5-minute walk from Odori, is where locals buy crab, scallops, salmon roe (ikura), and sea urchin (uni). Winter is peak season for taraba-gani (king crab) and ke-gani (hairy crab). Many stalls offer kaisendon (seafood rice bowls) for breakfast. Eat kaisendon at 8:00 AM, walk off your meal through the city, and eat ramen for lunch. This is the ideal Sapporo food day.

Odori Park and Sapporo TV Tower

The central green spine of the city, Odori Park is stunning under winter snow and is the main venue for the Snow Festival. The TV Tower at its eastern end offers panoramic views of the city blanketed in white. The park is sandwiched between ramen districts — Tanukikoji to the south, Sapporo Station to the north.

Sapporo Beer Museum and Beer Garden

Sapporo is Japan’s beer city, and the historic beer museum (free admission for the self-guided tour; ¥500 for the premium guided tasting) is housed in a beautiful red-brick building from 1890. The attached beer garden serves Genghis Khan (jingisukan) — grilled lamb on a dome-shaped grill, a Hokkaido specialty that is the perfect counterpart to miso ramen. Eat ramen for lunch, jingisukan for dinner. Your winter calorie intake will be heroic.

Moerenuma Park — Isamu Noguchi’s Masterpiece

The late sculptor Isamu Noguchi designed this entire park as a single work of art. In winter, it becomes a surreal snowscape. The glass pyramid (Hidamari) is heated and makes a beautiful rest stop. Take the Toho bus from Sapporo Station (about 25 minutes). Pair with a bowl of ramen in the Kita-guchi area when you return.

Day Trip: Otaru (30-40 minutes by train)

The charming canal town of Otaru, a quick JR train ride west of Sapporo along the coast, is famous for its glasswork, music boxes, canal-side architecture, and seafood. Otaru has its own excellent ramen scene, and visiting gives you a chance to compare styles. The train ride along the coast offers stunning winter ocean views. Go in the morning, eat sushi for lunch (Otaru’s sushi-ya are legendary), return to Sapporo for evening ramen.

Jozankei Onsen (Hot Spring Town) — 1 hour by bus

Soaking in a rotenburo (outdoor hot spring bath) while snow falls around you, then eating a bowl of miso ramen back in the city, is one of the most perfectly Hokkaido winter days imaginable. Direct buses run from Sapporo Station to Jozankei. Some ryokan (inns) offer day-use bathing for ¥800–¥1,500.

Getting There and Around

Getting to Sapporo

By air: New Chitose Airport (CTS) is Sapporo’s main airport, with direct flights from Tokyo Haneda/Narita (1 hour 45 minutes), Osaka Kansai (2 hours 10 minutes), and several international cities including Seoul, Taipei, Bangkok, and seasonal routes from other Asian hubs. The JR Rapid Airport train connects New Chitose to JR Sapporo Station in 37 minutes (¥1,150). Trains run every 15 minutes throughout the day.

By train from Tokyo: The Hokkaido Shinkansen runs from Tokyo to Shin-Hakodate-Hokuto (about 4 hours 20 minutes), where you transfer to the limited express Hokuto to Sapporo (about 3.5 hours). Total journey: approximately 8 hours. This route is covered by the Japan Rail Pass and is scenic but long. Most travelers fly.

By train from other Hokkaido cities: JR limited express trains connect Sapporo to Asahikawa (1 hour 25 minutes), Hakodate (3.5 hours), and Obihiro (2.5 hours).

Getting Around Sapporo

Subway: Sapporo’s three-line subway system is clean, efficient, and connects all major ramen districts. The Nanboku Line (north-south) and Tozai Line (east-west) cover most tourist needs. Single rides cost ¥210–¥320. A one-day pass (Donichika Kippu) costs ¥520 and is valid on weekends, holidays, and every day from December to March — this is the best deal in Sapporo winter travel.

Walking: Central Sapporo is built on a grid system (numbered blocks, like American cities) and is very walkable. However, in deep winter, sidewalks can be icy and snow-packed. Wear waterproof boots with good traction. Locals walk with a flat-footed, slightly penguin-like gait on icy surfaces — it works.

Underground passages: Sapporo has an extensive underground pedestrian network connecting Sapporo Station to Susukino (about 1.5 km). In harsh weather, you can walk the entire central ramen circuit without stepping outside. Access points are clearly marked.

Taxis: Readily available and honest (meters are always used). A ride from Sapporo Station to Susukino costs roughly ¥700–¥900. Useful for late-night ramen runs when the subway has stopped (last trains around 12:00 AM).

Where to Stay

Best Areas for Ramen Access

Susukino: The entertainment district puts you within a 5-minute walk of both Ramen Yokocho alleys and dozens of standalone shops. Hotel prices are moderate, and the area is safe (despite being the nightlife district). This is my top recommendation for ramen-focused trips.

Odori: Central, clean, and connected. You’re between the Susukino and Sapporo Station ramen zones, within easy walking distance of both. Slightly more upscale hotel options.

Sapporo Station (Kita-guchi): Best if you’re arriving late or departing early. Excellent ramen access on the north side, plus shopping and transit connections.

Budget

Look for business hotels in the Susukino or Tanukikoji area. Expect ¥5,000–¥8,000 per night for a clean single room. Capsule hotels are also available near Susukino Station for ¥3,000–¥4,500.

Mid-Range

Well-known Japanese business hotel chains cluster around Sapporo Station and Odori. Rooms run ¥8,000–¥15,000 and typically include breakfast. Many offer onsen (public bath) facilities on the top floor — invaluable after a day of walking in the cold.

Splurge

Several international and premium Japanese hotels occupy the Sapporo Station and Odori areas, with rooms from ¥20,000–¥45,000 per night. Winter rates can spike during Snow Festival week (early February) — book at least 2–3 months in advance for February visits.

👉 Book your Sapporo hotel early for winter travel. Snow Festival week (first week of February) sells out citywide. Even outside festival dates, winter weekend availability fills fast. Secure your accommodation as soon as your flights are confirmed.

Local Tips: Things Only Residents Know

1. The lunch vs. dinner divide is real. Many of Sapporo’s best ramen shops serve subtly different bowls at lunch and dinner. Lunch broth is often from the first, freshest batch of the day. Dinner broth has been simmering longer and is sometimes deeper in flavor. Neither is objectively “better,” but asking a local when to visit their favorite shop will often yield a specific answer — respect it.

2. Don’t overlook shoyu ramen in Sapporo. Yes, miso is the icon. But Sapporo has a quietly excellent shoyu ramen tradition that most tourists ignore entirely. If you eat ramen more than once during your trip (and you should), make at least one bowl shoyu. You may be surprised.

3. The “close the lid” trick for takeout cup ramen is amateur hour. If you want to bring Sapporo ramen home, skip the instant versions at the airport and instead visit the ramen shops that sell their own fresh noodle and soup kits (nama ramen sets, 生ラーメンセット). These are available at New Chitose Airport’s shopping area and at Sapporo Station’s food floors. They require refrigeration and have short shelf lives, but the quality is infinitely better than instant. Pack them in your carry-on for same-day return flights.

4. Counter seat etiquette matters. If you’re solo, sit at the counter. If there are two of you, sit at the counter if space allows and leave tables for groups. In tiny shops with only 8 seats, occupying two counter spots as a couple is normal and expected. Never place bags on empty seats during busy periods.

5. The master remembers. In small shops, the ramen master often recognizes repeat customers and may adjust your bowl slightly based on past visits. Even if you’re visiting for the first time, saying “hajimete desu” (this is my first time) with a smile often earns you a little extra care in your bowl.

6. The snow walk is part of the experience. Resist the urge to taxi everywhere. Walking through Sapporo’s snow-covered streets, ducking under icicle-hung eaves, and following the warm light of a ramen shop’s noren curtain is an aesthetic experience that defines the winter ramen pilgrimage. Dress properly (layers, waterproof boots, warm gloves) and embrace it.

7. Ramen breakfast exists. A handful of shops near Nijo Market and Susukino open as early as 7:00 or 8:00 AM. Eating miso ramen for breakfast on a freezing Sapporo morning is not weird — it’s wisdom.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Sapporo ramen always miso-based?

No. While miso ramen is Sapporo’s signature and most famous style, the city has excellent shoyu (soy sauce) and shio (salt) ramen as well. Many shops offer all three bases. However, if it’s your first time in Sapporo, start with miso — it’s the style that was born here and the one that local masters have perfected over decades.
