Best Wagyu Beef in Japan: Where to Eat Guide

Last updated: March 30, 2026

Best Wagyu Beef in Japan: Where to Eat Guide for the Ultimate Beef Experience

There is no food experience on earth quite like eating authentic wagyu beef in Japan. The marbling, the melt-on-your-tongue texture, the clean richness that lingers without heaviness — it’s a revelation that ruins every steak you’ll eat for the rest of your life. If you’re searching for the best wagyu beef in Japan where to eat guide, you’ve come to the right place. After 15 years of living here and eating my way through every major wagyu-producing region, I’ve put together this comprehensive guide to help you navigate the wonderful, occasionally confusing, and deeply rewarding world of Japanese beef.

Whether you’re a first-timer nervously eyeing a menu written entirely in kanji or a returning visitor ready to graduate from Kobe beef to lesser-known regional brands, this guide covers everything: where to go, what to order, how much to spend, when the beef is at its peak, and the practical tips that will save you from tourist traps and help you eat like a local.

Let’s get into it.

What Makes Japanese Wagyu Beef So Special? The Food Explained

A Brief History

The word “wagyu” (和牛) simply means “Japanese cow.” But there’s nothing simple about it. Japan’s relationship with beef is surprisingly recent. For over 1,000 years — from the 7th century through the Meiji Restoration in 1868 — eating meat from four-legged animals was largely taboo, influenced by Buddhist teachings and Shinto beliefs. Cattle were working animals, used for agriculture and transport.

When Japan opened to the West in the late 19th century, beef-eating became fashionable, even patriotic. The government actively encouraged it as a path to modernization. Suddenly, the cattle breeds that had been isolated in different mountain valleys across Japan — each developing distinct genetic characteristics over centuries — became prized for their meat rather than their labor.

This long genetic isolation is the secret behind wagyu’s extraordinary diversity. The four main breeds are:

  • Japanese Black (Kuroge Washu) — Accounts for roughly 90% of all wagyu. This is what you’ll eat most often, and it’s the breed behind Kobe, Matsusaka, Omi, and most other famous brands.
  • Japanese Brown (Akage Washu) — Leaner, with a beefier, more robust flavor. Found mainly in Kumamoto and Kochi prefectures.
  • Japanese Shorthorn (Nihon Tankaku) — Raised primarily in northern Tohoku. Lean, grass-fed flavor with less marbling.
  • Japanese Polled (Mukaku Washu) — Extremely rare, fewer than 200 animals exist. You’re unlikely to encounter it.

The Grading System

Japan’s beef grading system is the most rigorous in the world. The Japan Meat Grading Association (JMGA) evaluates carcasses on two scales:

  • Yield grade (A, B, or C) — How much usable meat comes from the carcass. A is the highest.
  • Quality grade (1-5) — Based on marbling, color, firmness, and fat quality. 5 is the highest.

So A5 is the pinnacle: maximum yield, maximum quality. But here’s a local tip that most tourists don’t know: A4 wagyu is often more enjoyable to eat in larger portions. A5 is so intensely marbled that many Japanese people prefer it in small quantities — a few slices of sashimi-style beef, or two to three pieces of yakiniku. If you’re ordering a full steak, A4 gives you that extraordinary wagyu experience with a slightly better balance of meat flavor to fat.

Regional Brands: The Big Three and Beyond

Japan has over 300 regional wagyu brands (銘柄牛, meigara-gyu). The most famous are:

  • Kobe Beef (神戸ビーフ) — From Hyogo Prefecture’s Tajima strain of Japanese Black cattle. Strict certification requirements. The most famous internationally, though not necessarily the “best.”
  • Matsusaka Beef (松阪牛) — From Mie Prefecture. Only virgin female cows qualify. Renowned for its sweetness and incredibly fine marbling. Often considered the connoisseur’s choice.
  • Omi Beef (近江牛) — From Shiga Prefecture. Japan’s oldest beef brand, with over 400 years of history. Rich, delicate, and slightly less fatty than Kobe.

Beyond the Big Three, these regional brands deserve your attention:

  • Yonezawa Beef (米沢牛) — From Yamagata Prefecture. One of Japan’s top three wagyu brands by most rankings. Exceptional sweetness.
  • Hida Beef (飛騨牛) — From Gifu Prefecture. Beautiful marbling with a clean finish. Often more affordable than the big names.
  • Miyazaki Beef (宮崎牛) — From Miyazaki Prefecture in Kyushu. Has won the “Wagyu Olympics” (held every five years) multiple times. Stunning quality.
  • Saga Beef (佐賀牛) — Another Kyushu powerhouse. Rich, buttery, and underappreciated outside Japan.

Best Places to Eat Wagyu in Japan: 5 Essential Destinations

Finding the best wagyu beef in Japan where to eat guide recommendations requires looking beyond the obvious. Here are the areas and districts where you’ll find the highest concentration of excellent wagyu restaurants.

1. Kobe — Kitano-cho and Sannomiya Districts (Hyogo Prefecture)

The name that started it all for international visitors. Kobe’s Sannomiya station area and the upscale Kitano-cho district are packed with teppanyaki restaurants serving certified Kobe beef. The classic Kobe experience is sitting at a teppanyaki counter, watching a chef cook your steak on a steel griddle with mesmerizing precision.

What to look for: Restaurants displaying the official Kobe Beef certification mark (a chrysanthemum-shaped logo). The Kobe Beef Marketing & Distribution Promotion Association maintains a list of certified restaurants on their website — check it before you go. Avoid any restaurant near the harbor area that’s aggressively soliciting tourists in English; these are often serving generic wagyu at Kobe prices.

Local tip: Many Kobe locals actually prefer to eat Kobe beef as shabu-shabu (swished briefly through hot broth) rather than steak. The thin slicing and gentle cooking lets you taste the sweetness of the fat without the searing. Ask for it.

2. Matsusaka City and Tsu City (Mie Prefecture)

Matsusaka is a quiet castle town about 90 minutes from Nagoya or Osaka, and it’s a pilgrimage site for serious beef lovers. The concentration of wagyu restaurants along Matsusaka’s main shopping streets near the station is remarkable for such a small city. This is where you can eat what many Japanese food critics consider the finest beef in the country.

What to order: Matsusaka beef sukiyaki is the quintessential preparation here. The beef is simmered in a sweet soy-based sauce with tofu, vegetables, and noodles, then dipped in raw beaten egg. It sounds simple. It will change your understanding of what beef can be.

Local tip: The nearby city of Tsu also has excellent Matsusaka beef restaurants, often at lower prices than Matsusaka city itself, since it’s not the “brand name” town.

3. Kyoto — Gion, Pontocho, and Kawaramachi Districts

Kyoto isn’t a cattle-producing region, but its centuries-old culinary culture means the city has some of the most refined wagyu dining in Japan. The narrow streets of Pontocho and the geisha district of Gion hide intimate kaiseki restaurants and steak houses that source top-grade Omi beef from neighboring Shiga Prefecture or Kobe beef from Hyogo.

What makes it special: Kyoto chefs tend to treat wagyu as one element within a multi-course kaiseki meal, meaning you’ll get smaller, perfectly portioned servings alongside seasonal vegetables, tofu, and pickles. It’s wagyu in context, not isolation, and it’s magnificent.

Local tip: The Kawaramachi/Shijo area has a broader range of price points, including excellent yakiniku restaurants where you can grill your own wagyu for roughly half the price of a Gion teppanyaki experience.

4. Tokyo — Shibuya, Ginza, Shinjuku, and Asakusa

Tokyo is where every regional wagyu brand competes for attention. This is both a blessing and a challenge — the options are overwhelming.

  • Ginza is where you’ll find the highest-end teppanyaki and steak experiences, often in department store upper floors or quiet basement restaurants.
  • Shibuya and Shinjuku have the widest range, from budget yakiniku joints to mid-range steak restaurants.
  • Asakusa and the eastern side of the city tend to offer more traditional preparations like sukiyaki and shabu-shabu.

What makes it special: In Tokyo, you can compare Kobe, Matsusaka, Yonezawa, Miyazaki, and Hida beef all within a single district. Some restaurants even offer tasting comparisons of different brands.

Local tip: For the best value wagyu in Tokyo, head to the yakiniku restaurants under the train tracks in Yurakucho and Shinbashi (the area known as “Gado-shita”). These no-frills spots serve excellent quality beef in a lively, smoky atmosphere at prices that would shock Ginza diners. Lunch sets throughout Tokyo also offer dramatic savings — the same restaurant charging ¥15,000 for dinner might have a ¥3,000-¥5,000 wagyu lunch course.

5. Takayama and the Hida Region (Gifu Prefecture)

The mountain town of Takayama is famous for its Edo-period streetscapes, morning markets, and Hida beef — a wagyu brand that punches well above its weight. Walking down Kamisannomachi Street (the old town), you’ll find Hida beef served as skewers, sushi (yes, beef sushi — thin slices of seared wagyu on vinegared rice), croquettes, and buns.

What makes it special: The casual street-food format makes Hida beef accessible and fun. You can try a ¥500-800 wagyu beef sushi piece as a snack between temple visits, or sit down for a full Hida beef course at dinner.

Local tip: Hida beef nigiri sushi from the street vendors in old town Takayama is one of the best food-to-price ratios in all of wagyu tourism. Two pieces for ¥600-1,000. Eat it on the spot.

6. Miyazaki City and the Kirishima Area (Miyazaki Prefecture)

If you’re venturing to Kyushu, Miyazaki is a wagyu destination that most international visitors overlook entirely. Miyazaki beef has won the prestigious National Wagyu Ability Expo (the so-called “Wagyu Olympics”) in multiple recent competitions, and the quality is extraordinary.

What makes it special: Prices in Miyazaki are significantly lower than in Kobe or Tokyo for comparable (or superior) quality. A full Miyazaki A5 wagyu course dinner might cost 30-50% less than an equivalent meal in Ginza.

Local tip: Don’t skip Miyazaki’s chicken nanban while you’re here — the prefecture is equally famous for its chicken dishes, and the contrast makes the wagyu taste even more special.

7. Yonezawa (Yamagata Prefecture)

Deep in the mountains of southern Yamagata, Yonezawa is a former castle town with one of Japan’s most revered beef brands. Yonezawa beef is characterized by its fine-grained marbling and a distinctive sweetness that comes from the cattle’s diet and the cold mountain climate.

What makes it special: Yonezawa is a true off-the-beaten-path destination. You’ll likely be the only international visitor in the restaurant. The town also produces excellent ramen, sake, and onsen (hot spring) experiences.

Local tip: Combine Yonezawa beef with a visit to the nearby Zao Onsen hot springs. Having a hot spring bath before a wagyu dinner is peak Japan.

Best Time to Visit for Wagyu: Seasonal Availability and Quality

Unlike seafood or produce, wagyu beef is available year-round. Cattle are raised and processed throughout the year, so you won’t encounter an “off season.” That said, there are meaningful seasonal considerations:

Month-by-Month Breakdown

Month Notes
January–February Peak season for sukiyaki and shabu-shabu — hot pot dishes are most popular in cold weather. Many restaurants feature special winter courses. Yonezawa and Takayama are particularly atmospheric in snow.
March–April Cherry blossom season. Kyoto and Tokyo wagyu restaurants are busiest. Book 2-4 weeks ahead for popular Gion or Ginza spots. The combination of hanami (flower viewing) and wagyu is sublime.
May–June Excellent time to visit. Crowds thin after Golden Week (early May). Early summer menus appear with lighter preparations. The rainy season (mid-June) keeps tourist numbers low — great for walk-in availability.
July–August Summer heat makes Japanese diners prefer lighter fare, which means yakiniku (grilled at your table) peaks in popularity. Beer gardens serving wagyu yakiniku appear on department store rooftops in major cities. Some dedicated wagyu restaurants offer summer-only cold beef dishes (冷しゃぶ, reishabu — chilled shabu-shabu).
September–October Arguably the best time for wagyu dining overall. Autumn appetite returns, new seasonal menus launch, and the weather is perfect for travel. Matsutake mushrooms paired with wagyu in October is a bucket-list combination. Takayama’s famous autumn festival falls in October.
November–December Year-end party season (bonenkai) means restaurants are packed, especially on Friday and Saturday nights in December. Book well ahead. Winter wagyu courses are often the most elaborate, with truffles, seasonal root vegetables, and special cuts.

The bottom line: The best time to eat wagyu in Japan is whenever you’re here. But autumn (September–November) offers the ideal combination of comfortable weather, seasonal menu highlights, and manageable crowds.

How to Order and Eat Wagyu: A Practical Guide for First-Timers

Walking into a wagyu restaurant for the first time can be intimidating. Here’s everything you need to know.

Choosing Your Preparation Style

  • Teppanyaki (鉄板焼き) — Chef cooks your steak on a flat iron griddle in front of you. The most “Western” experience. Great for steak lovers.
  • Yakiniku (焼肉) — Korean-style BBQ where you grill thin-sliced beef over charcoal at your table. The most interactive and often the best value.
  • Sukiyaki (すき焼き) — Thin-sliced beef simmered in sweet soy broth, dipped in raw egg. Deeply traditional.
  • Shabu-shabu (しゃぶしゃぶ) — Paper-thin beef swished in hot broth for seconds, then dipped in ponzu or sesame sauce. The purest way to taste the beef’s quality.
  • Steak (ステーキ) — Western-style steak, often smaller portions (100-200g) than you’d expect. Served with wasabi and soy sauce or garlic chips.
  • Gyudon/Beef bowl (牛丼) — Simmered beef over rice. Not typically wagyu, but some specialty shops use A4-A5 grade beef. Incredible value when you find it.
  • Beef sushi (牛寿司) — Seared or raw wagyu on sushi rice. A newer trend but wonderful.

Ordering Tips

  1. Start with a course meal (コース, kōsu). Most wagyu restaurants offer set courses that include appetizers, salad, soup, rice, and dessert alongside the beef. These are almost always better value and better curated than à la carte ordering.

  2. Don’t order the biggest portion of A5. Seriously. 100-150 grams of A5 wagyu steak is plenty for most people. The richness is intense. If you want more meat, order A4, or mix A5 with leaner cuts.

  3. At yakiniku, order a variety of cuts. Don’t just get sirloin. Try tongue (tan), skirt (harami), and short rib (karubi) alongside the premium cuts. The contrast teaches you about the animal.

  4. Lunch sets are your secret weapon. The same restaurant that charges ¥20,000+ for dinner often has lunch courses for ¥3,000-¥8,000, sometimes with identical beef.

  5. Say “osusume wa nan desu ka?” (おすすめは何ですか？) — “What do you recommend?” This works everywhere and often leads to the chef’s proudest offering.

Price Guide: What to Expect at Every Budget

Budget: ¥1,500-¥4,000 per person

  • Yakiniku lunch sets
  • Wagyu beef bowls (gyudon) at specialty shops
  • Wagyu street food in Takayama (skewers, sushi, croquettes)
  • Department store basement (depachika) wagyu bento boxes
  • Standing steak bars in Tokyo (Shibuya, Shinjuku)

Mid-Range: ¥5,000-¥12,000 per person

  • Dinner yakiniku courses with A4-A5 beef
  • Lunch teppanyaki courses at upscale restaurants
  • Sukiyaki or shabu-shabu dinner courses
  • Regional restaurants in Matsusaka, Yonezawa, or Miyazaki

Splurge: ¥15,000-¥40,000+ per person

  • Full dinner teppanyaki courses in Ginza or Gion
  • Certified Kobe beef teppanyaki experiences
  • Multi-course kaiseki meals featuring wagyu as the centerpiece
  • Private room dining with premium sake pairings

Reality check: You can have an extraordinary, life-changing wagyu experience for ¥5,000-¥8,000 at the right place and time. You do not need to spend ¥30,000 unless you want the full luxury theater of a Ginza teppanyaki counter. Some of my best wagyu meals in 15 years have been at mid-range yakiniku joints and small-town restaurants.

What to Eat Beyond Wagyu: Seasonal and Regional Pairings

A great wagyu meal is even better when paired with seasonal Japanese food and drink. Here’s what to look for:

  • Spring: Bamboo shoots (takenoko), rapeseed blossoms (nanohana), fresh mountain vegetables (sansai). Many kaiseki courses pair wagyu with these.
  • Summer: Cold tofu (hiyayakko), edamame, and draft beer. Chilled wagyu shabu-shabu (reishabu) is a summer delicacy.
  • Autumn: Matsutake mushrooms grilled alongside wagyu. New-harvest rice (shinmai). Japanese sweet potato. This is the peak season for food in Japan.
  • Winter: Hot pot combinations, root vegetables, and yuzu citrus-based sauces. Rich, warming flavors complement the beef’s fat beautifully.

Drink pairings: Japanese sake (particularly a dry junmai style) or Japanese whisky (a Yamazaki or Hakushu highball) pairs beautifully with wagyu. Surprisingly, Japanese craft beer — especially malty amber ales — is also excellent. In Kobe, local sake from the Nada district is the classic pairing.

Nearby Sights to Combine with Your Wagyu Trip

From Kobe

  • Arima Onsen — One of Japan’s oldest hot springs, just 30 minutes from Kobe by train. Soak in iron-rich “gold” waters after your beef dinner.
  • Himeji Castle — Japan’s most spectacular original castle, 40 minutes west by shinkansen.
  • Kobe Harborland — Waterfront shopping and night views of the port.

From Matsusaka

  • Ise Grand Shrine — Japan’s most sacred Shinto shrine, 30 minutes away. Combine a spiritual morning at Ise with an afternoon Matsusaka beef lunch.
  • Toba — Pearl island and aquarium on the coast.

From Kyoto

  • Kyoto itself is the sight. Combine a morning at Fushimi Inari Shrine or Arashiyama Bamboo Grove with an evening wagyu kaiseki in Gion. Pure perfection.

From Takayama

  • Shirakawa-go — UNESCO World Heritage thatched-roof villages, one hour by bus.
  • Kamikochi — Stunning alpine valley in the Japanese Alps (open April-November).

From Miyazaki

  • Takachiho Gorge — Dramatic river gorge with rowboat rides and mythology connections.
  • Aoshima Shrine — Subtropical island shrine surrounded by unique “Devil’s Washboard” rock formations.

Practical Transport Tips

  • A Japan Rail Pass covers travel to Kobe, Kyoto, Takayama, and Yonezawa from Tokyo.
  • Matsusaka is most easily reached via Kintetsu Railway from Osaka-Namba or Nagoya (not covered by JR Pass, but affordable).
  • Miyazaki is best reached by domestic flight from Tokyo (Haneda) or Osaka (Itami/Kansai). Budget airlines like Solaseed Air and Peach operate these routes.
  • Within each city, wagyu districts are typically walkable from the main train station. Kobe (Sannomiya), Takayama, Matsusaka, and Yonezawa all have restaurants within 5-15 minutes on foot from the station.

Where to Stay

Kobe

Stay near Sannomiya Station for the best access to wagyu restaurants. This is also Kobe’s main shopping and nightlife area. Mid-range business hotels offer excellent value and put you within stumbling distance of your teppanyaki dinner.

Kyoto

Gion, Kawaramachi, or Pontocho-adjacent hotels let you walk to wagyu restaurants after a day of temple-hopping. A traditional ryokan (Japanese inn) in eastern Kyoto adds another layer to the culinary experience.

Takayama

Stay in a ryokan with half-board (dinner and breakfast included) — many serve Hida beef as the centerpiece of their kaiseki dinner. This is one of the best ways to experience wagyu: in a tatami room, wearing a yukata, after a hot spring bath.

Tokyo

Shibuya, Shinjuku, or Ginza give you the most wagyu restaurant options within walking distance. Tokyo has excellent value business hotels across all these areas.

👉 Book your Japan accommodation early, especially during cherry blossom season (late March–mid April) and autumn foliage season (November). Popular ryokan in Takayama and Yonezawa can sell out months in advance.

Local Tips: Things Only Residents Know

  1. Google Maps is your best restaurant-finding tool in Japan. Search the Japanese term (e.g., “神戸ビーフ ステーキ” for Kobe beef steak) and filter by rating. Japanese users leave detailed, honest reviews. Any restaurant above 3.5 stars on Google Maps in Japan is genuinely good; above 4.0 is exceptional.

  2. Tabelog (tabelog.com) is the local equivalent of Yelp and even more trusted by Japanese diners. Scores above 3.5 on Tabelog indicate a top-tier restaurant. The site has an English version, though the Japanese version has more reviews.

  3. “Wagyu” at tourist-trap restaurants might be dairy cow crossbreeds. Legally, the term “wagyu” in Japan is tightly controlled, but some restaurants serving foreign tourists play fast and loose with descriptions. Look for the specific brand name (Kobe, Matsusaka, Hida, etc.) and the JMGA grade certificate, which reputable restaurants display proudly — often framed near the entrance or shown on a card at your table with the individual animal’s nose print and identification number.

  4. Department store basement food halls (depachika) sell wagyu bento boxes, wagyu croquettes, and ready-to-eat wagyu dishes for a fraction of restaurant prices. Isetan in Shinjuku, Takashimaya in Nihonbashi, and Daimaru in Kobe are excellent options.

  5. Avoid restaurants with English-speaking touts outside. In Kobe especially, there’s an unfortunate concentration of overpriced, mediocre restaurants near tourist areas that employ people to shepherd visitors inside. The best wagyu restaurants don’t need touts — they have waiting lists.

  6. Wagyu fat coats your palate. Eat pickled ginger (gari), grated daikon, or pickled vegetables between courses to cleanse your palate. Most good restaurants provide these automatically, but don’t be shy about asking for more.

  7. Conveyor belt (kaitenzushi) restaurants sometimes offer wagyu sushi as a seasonal or premium plate. It won’t be A5, but at ¥300-500 per plate, it’s a fun and accessible introduction.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Kobe beef really the best wagyu in Japan?

Kobe beef is the most famous wagyu internationally, but most Japanese food critics and beef enthusiasts would rank Matsusaka and Miyazaki alongside or above it. Kobe beef is excellent, but its fame is partly due to early international marketing. Don’t limit yourself — exploring regional brands like Yonezawa, Hida, and Saga beef is one of the great pleasures of eating wagyu in Japan.

How much should I budget for a wagyu meal?

You can have an excellent wagyu experience for ¥3,000-5,000 at a yakiniku lunch or standing steak bar. A memorable teppanyaki dinner at a quality restaurant runs ¥10,000-20,000. Ultra-premium experiences in Ginza or Gion start at ¥25,000+. My recommendation: allocate ¥8,000-15,000 for one special wagyu dinner and try cheaper formats (street food, lunch sets, yakiniku) for other meals.

Do I need reservations?

For mid-range and upscale restaurants, yes — especially on weekends and during peak seasons (cherry blossom, autumn foliage, year-end). Many restaurants accept reservations through their websites, Tabelog, or phone. If you don’t speak Japanese, ask your hotel concierge to call for you — this is a completely normal and expected service in Japan. Some restaurants also accept reservations through services like TableCheck or Pocket Concierge (English-friendly).

Is wagyu safe to eat rare or raw?

Yes. Japan’s food safety standards for beef are extremely strict. Wagyu steak is typically served medium-rare to medium, and beef sushi (lightly seared or raw) is widely enjoyed. The only exception: raw beef liver has been banned in Japan since 2012 due to food safety concerns. Everything else — rare steak, beef tartare, beef sushi — is safe at reputable restaurants.

Can I bring wagyu beef home as a souvenir?

This depends entirely on your home country’s import regulations. The United States, Australia, and most EU countries prohibit importing meat products due to biosecurity concerns. You will have it confiscated at customs and may face fines. Enjoy it in Japan and bring home vacuum-packed wagyu curry, wagyu-flavored snacks, or beef-flavored furikake (rice seasoning) instead — these processed items generally clear customs without issue.

What’s the difference between wagyu and “WAGYU” sold outside Japan?

Many countries now raise cattle from Japanese genetics and market the beef as “wagyu.” Australian wagyu, American wagyu, and others can be very good, but they’re raised differently (diet, environment, duration of feeding) and lack the Japanese grading system’s rigor. The flavor profile and marbling intensity are noticeably different. If you’re in Japan, eat Japanese wagyu — you’ll understand the difference immediately.

I don’t eat large portions of rich food. Can I still enjoy wagyu?

Absolutely. In fact, this is the ideal way to eat wagyu. Order beef sushi (1-2 pieces), share a small steak, or opt for shabu-shabu where you control exactly how much you eat. Many course meals include only 80-120 grams of beef alongside lighter dishes. The Japanese approach to wagyu has always been about quality over quantity — a few perfect bites rather than a massive slab. Embrace that philosophy, and you’ll have a better experience.

Japan’s wagyu tradition is one of the world’s great culinary achievements — a story of isolated genetics, obsessive craftsmanship, and deep regional pride distilled into every bite. Whether you’re eating a ¥600 beef sushi piece on a Takayama street corner or a ¥30,000 kaiseki course in a Kyoto machiya, you’re participating in something extraordinary. Go hungry, stay curious, and don’t fill up on rice before the beef arrives.
