Gion Guide: Kyoto's Geisha District & How to Visit Respectfully

Last updated: June 20, 2026

Gion is Kyoto’s most famous geisha district — a maze of wooden machiya townhouses, paper-lantern-lit lanes, exclusive tea houses, and willow-lined canals, where the city’s living traditions of music, dance, and hospitality have continued for centuries. At dusk, when the lanterns glow and a geiko (Kyoto’s word for geisha) or maiko (apprentice) hurries to an evening engagement, Gion feels like stepping into another era.

It is also a real, working neighbourhood where over-tourism and disrespectful behaviour have caused genuine problems — to the point that some private lanes now ban photography entirely. This guide covers what to see, how the geisha world actually works, where to experience it properly, and — importantly — how to visit Gion respectfully.

🗓️ Quick Reference
Location Gion, Higashiyama-ku, Kyoto (around Shijo & the Kamo River)
Cost Free to stroll; performances and dinners vary
Nearest station Gion-Shijo (Keihan) / Kawaramachi (Hankyu)
Best time Early evening (dusk) for atmosphere
Photography Public streets OK; private lanes restricted — watch signs

Geiko & Maiko: What You’re Actually Seeing

In Kyoto, a fully-fledged geisha is called a geiko, and her teenage apprentice is a maiko. They are highly trained professional artists — masters of traditional dance, the shamisen, song, tea, and the art of conversation and hospitality. They are not tourist performers or, despite persistent foreign myths, anything to do with the sex trade.

Telling them apart:

  • Maiko (apprentice): elaborate, colourful kimono with very long, trailing sleeves; ornate hair with dangling kanzashi ornaments and seasonal flowers; tall wooden okobo sandals; often younger.
  • Geiko (full geisha): more subdued, elegant kimono; a wig rather than styled natural hair; simpler ornamentation; refined rather than showy.

Important reality check: Most “geiko” you see being photographed on the streets in daytime are actually tourists who have paid for a maiko/geiko makeover and kimono dress-up (see our maiko makeover guide). Real geiko and maiko are usually seen only briefly in the early evening, moving quickly between appointments — they are at work, not on display.

The Two Essential Lanes

Hanamikoji-dori

The most famous street in Gion, running south from Shijo, lined with exclusive ochaya (tea houses) and high-end traditional restaurants behind wooden facades and noren curtains. It’s beautiful — but it is a private street, and photography is officially banned on the private lanes off Hanamikoji, with fines for violations. Walk it, admire it, but put the camera away where signs require.

Shirakawa (Gion Shirakawa)

Quieter and arguably more beautiful: a willow-lined canal with stone-paved walkways, old wooden teahouses, and little bridges. In cherry-blossom season it’s magical. This area is more relaxed for respectful photography of the scenery (not of people without consent).

Photography & Etiquette — Please Read

Gion has become a flashpoint for over-tourism. The local community has imposed real rules:

  • Private lanes off Hanamikoji ban photography entirely, with signage and fines.
  • Never photograph geiko or maiko without permission. Do not chase, touch, block, or grab them for photos — this has been a serious problem and is deeply disrespectful. They are professionals on their way to work.
  • Stay on public streets. Do not enter private property or peer into tea houses.
  • Don’t eat while walking on the historic lanes; dispose of rubbish properly.
  • Treat Gion as someone’s neighbourhood and workplace, because that is exactly what it is.

Visiting respectfully isn’t just politeness — it’s the difference between this living tradition surviving tourism or being driven behind closed doors.

How to Experience Geisha Culture Properly

Rather than hunting for a street glimpse, see the real art in a proper setting:

  • Public dance performances: The geiko and maiko communities hold annual public dances — the Miyako Odori (April) and Kamogawa Odori (May) are spectacular, ticketed stage performances, the best and most respectful way to see their art. See our Kyoto festivals guide.
  • Gion Corner: A theatre offering an accessible introductory show of Kyoto arts (dance, tea, music) aimed at visitors.
  • Maiko dinners & ozashiki experiences: Some venues and tour operators arrange dinners or tea gatherings with a maiko, where you can watch a dance, play games, and ask questions — a genuine, sanctioned encounter (book in advance; not cheap).

Best Time to Visit

  • Early evening (dusk): The most atmospheric — lanterns lit, the chance (no guarantee) of glimpsing a real geiko heading to work. Stroll Shirakawa and Hanamikoji quietly.
  • Early morning: Quiet, beautiful light, empty lanes for scenery photography.
  • Avoid: Mid-day weekend crowds, and never come purely to “hunt” geiko with a camera.

Combining Your Visit

Gion is the northern end of the great Higashiyama walking route:

  • South: Yasaka Shrine → Yasaka Pagoda → Ninenzaka/Sannenzaka → Kiyomizu-dera — see our Kiyomizu-dera guide.
  • Pontocho: Across the river, a narrow lane of restaurants and another geisha district, lovely at night.
  • Nishiki Market and central dining are a short walk/tram away — see our Nishiki Market guide.

Getting There

  • Keihan Line → Gion-Shijo Station, right at the edge of the district.
  • Hankyu Line → Kawaramachi Station, then a short walk across the river.
  • Bus from Kyoto Station to the Gion stop on Shijo-dori.
