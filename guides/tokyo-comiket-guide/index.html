Every year, twice a year, the attention of the anime and manga world swings to a cluster of futuristic buildings on the waterfront of Tokyo Bay. Over three days, more than 700,000 fans pour into Tokyo Big Sight for Comiket — the world’s largest self-published media fair, where over 30,000 independent creator groups (circles) sell their own original comics, art books, games, and music directly to buyers. If Akihabara is anime culture’s permanent home, Comiket is its beating heart.

For overseas visitors, Comiket is also one of Tokyo’s most intimidating events to navigate — sprawling, crowd-intensive, conducted almost entirely in Japanese, and demanding serious preparation to enjoy properly. This guide covers everything you need to know.

⚠️ Summer Comiket Heat Warning — Read This First

August in Tokyo regularly hits 34–38°C (93–100°F) with 70–80% humidity. The outdoor queue area is exposed concrete with zero shade, and dozens of attendees are treated for heatstroke at every summer edition. Insufficient water, no electrolytes, denim jeans, and skipping breakfast are the four most common mistakes — all preventable.

📋 Pre-Departure Decision Checklist

Lock these decisions in before you leave for Japan — most are impossible to fix once you’ve arrived.

✈️ Trip-Level Decisions (4–6 weeks ahead)

  • Which edition? Summer (mid-August) or Winter (late December) — radically different climates
  • Which day(s)? Day 1, 2, or 3 — genre assignments are confirmed about 6–8 weeks out
  • Flight + hotel booked? Tokyo accommodation sells out months in advance during Obon (summer)
  • Hotel near a Rinkai or Yamanote Line station — minimises the morning commute when you’re sleep-deprived
  • Travel insurance that explicitly covers heatstroke / hospital treatment

📖 Catalog & Targets (3–4 weeks ahead)

  • Catalog acquired — Web Catalog subscription or physical DVD edition
  • Top 5–10 circles identified — your immediate priorities at opening
  • Top 20–30 circles marked for secondary visits if time allows
  • Corporate lottery entries submitted for any must-have exclusives (Type-Moon, Key, etc.)
  • Backup circles noted in case top targets sell out

🧳 Logistics (1–2 weeks ahead)

  • Cash withdrawal plan — know your bank’s daily limit and which Japanese ATMs accept your card
  • Pocket Wi-Fi or eSIM ordered for offline catalog app + maps
  • Hotel address in Japanese copied (photo of business card) for Yamato shipping slips
  • International shipping account set up (Tenso/Buyee) if you’ll exceed your luggage allowance
  • Heat gear sourced (or note convenience stores near hotel) — cooling towel, portable fan, salt candy

🌅 The Day Before

  • Cash withdrawn (¥20,000–50,000 from a 7-Eleven ATM)
  • Phone fully charged + battery pack topped up
  • Catalog app data downloaded offline — don’t rely on venue Wi-Fi
  • Train route memorised (Rinkai Line, NOT Yurikamome on event day)
  • Departure time set — aim to leave the hotel by 7:00 AM
  • Breakfast plan — light but solid; never skip in summer (konbini onigiri en route is fine)
  • Water + sports drink in your bag before you head out the door

🎌 What is Comiket?

Comic Market (コミックマーケット), universally called Comiket or C followed by its edition number, was founded in 1975 in Tokyo. What began as a gathering of 700 fans of self-published manga now spans hundreds of thousands of square metres of exhibition space and is held twice a year:

  • Summer Comiket: Three days in mid-August, during Japan’s Obon holiday season
  • Winter Comiket: Three to four days from around December 28–31

The event’s core is doujinshi (同人誌) — self-published works that range from fan comics set in existing franchises to completely original fiction, illustration books, novels, and music CDs. Every creator pays a circle participation fee and gets a small booth (typically one or two tables) to sell directly to buyers. No licencing, no retail markup, no middlemen.

Alongside these 30,000+ indie creators, the Corporate Area (企業ブース) hosts major anime studios, game companies, and publishers who launch exclusive merchandise, collaboration goods, and event-limited products — creating a secondary draw that rivals the circles themselves.

The result is a unique ecosystem: polished commercial releases alongside hand-stapled photocopied zines, all selling side by side in the same enormous hall.

📅 When & Where: Summer 2026

Venue: Tokyo Big Sight (東京ビッグサイト)
3-11-1 Ariake, Koto-ku, Tokyo

Summer Comiket 2026 is anticipated for mid-August 2026, likely August 9–11 or 10–12 (dates typically announced 4–6 months in advance). Check the official website for confirmed dates: www.comiket.co.jp

General hours: 10:00–16:00 (East / West / South halls may open in sequence). Queuing begins outside from approximately 6:00–7:00.

Important for travel planning: Summer Comiket falls during Japan’s peak domestic travel period (Obon). Book flights and hotels as soon as you confirm your dates — Tokyo accommodation during Obon sells out months in advance.

🚆 Getting There

Tokyo Big Sight is on Ariake island in Tokyo Bay, about 30–40 minutes from central Tokyo by train.

Rinkai Line (りんかい線) — Recommended

The Rinkai Line is the fastest and highest-capacity route on Comiket days.

Board at Osaki Station (change from JR Yamanote Line) and ride to Kokusai-tenjijo Station (国際展示場駅). The Big Sight is a 7-minute walk from the station exit — follow the crowds.

From Journey time Approx. fare
Shinjuku → Osaki (Yamanote) → Kokusai-tenjijo ~35 min ¥630
Shibuya → Osaki → Kokusai-tenjijo ~38 min ¥630
Ikebukuro → Osaki → Kokusai-tenjijo ~45 min ¥730

Suica/Pasmo IC cards are accepted on both lines.

Yurikamome (ゆりかもめ) — Scenic but unreliable on event days

The Yurikamome monorail runs from Shimbashi or Toyosu to Kokusai-tenjijomae Station (国際展示場正門駅), which deposits you directly at the Big Sight main gate.

⚠️ Warning: Yurikamome has significantly lower capacity than the Rinkai Line. On Comiket mornings, trains run on reduced headway and can be suspended or severely delayed between 9:00 and 11:00. Use the Rinkai Line on the day of the event.

Timing tip: Depart before 9:00 to reach the Big Sight before the worst crowd surge. Trains toward Ariake between 9:30 and 11:00 are extremely packed regardless of which line you take.

📖 Preparing Before You Go

Step 1: Get the Catalog

The Comiket Catalog (カタログ) is the event bible — listing every circle, their location, genre, and a small sample of their work.

  • Physical catalog (DVD edition): Available at major bookstores (Animate, Toranoana, Melonbooks, Mandarake) and convenience stores (7-Eleven, Lawson, FamilyMart) from approximately six weeks before the event. Price: ¥2,500–3,000. Includes a data DVD for PC-based browsing.
  • Web Catalog (ウェブカタログ): Free basic access at webcatalog.comiket.com . A paid subscription (~¥1,500) unlocks the circle-check feature and route planner.
  • Smartphone app: A companion app (iOS/Android) lets you browse the catalog, mark circles, and plot a route — download it before the event and set your checks while on Wi-Fi.

Step 2: Do Your Circle Checks

Browsing 30,000+ circles blind on the day is not feasible. Before you go:

  1. Browse the catalog (by genre, by day, or by keyword) and mark every circle whose work you want to see
  2. Note the block code for each circle — e.g. “東ヘ-12a” means East Hall (東), Block H (ヘ), Space 12a
  3. Prioritise your top 5–10 targets: these get visited immediately after opening; popular circles sell out within 20–60 minutes
  4. Use the app’s route planner to sequence your stops efficiently

Step 3: Withdraw Cash

Most circles are cash-only. Some have adopted PayPay or Square card readers, but you cannot rely on this — assume cash everywhere.

Recommended amounts:

Visit style Cash to bring
Casual browse, 1–2 circles ¥5,000–10,000
Focused visit, 10+ circles ¥20,000–30,000
Full-day collector mode ¥50,000+

ATMs at and near Tokyo Big Sight run out of cash by mid-morning on Comiket days. Withdraw in advance from a 7-Eleven ATM (the most internationally compatible ATM network in Japan). Do not wait until you arrive.

🗺️ Navigating the Venue

Tokyo Big Sight has several halls used during Comiket. The layout changes slightly each edition, but the general structure:

East Exhibition Halls (東展示棟)

The largest area, containing the majority of doujinshi circles. Halls are divided into blocks by Japanese phonetic character (A–Z in hiragana, then extended characters). Each block is a grid of tables; your circle-check map shows exactly where each circle is.

West Exhibition Halls (西展示棟)

Typically hosts part of the Corporate Area (企業ブース) — booths operated by major game companies, anime studios, and publishers. Also used for certain circle genres depending on the edition.

South Exhibition Hall (南展示棟)

A newer addition to Big Sight, used for overflow circles, specific genres, or additional corporate exhibitors. Always check the current edition’s hall map before going.

Day-by-Day Genre Assignments

Each day is assigned to specific genres. The traditional pattern:

  • Day 1 & 2: Female-oriented genres — Boys' Love (BL/腐女子向け), female-targeted fandoms, certain general genres
  • Day 3: Male-oriented genres — moe, military, video games, certain general genres; Corporate Area tends to be busiest

This assignment changes every edition. Always confirm the actual day breakdown from the official catalog or website for the specific edition you’re attending.

🛍️ Buying at Circle Spaces

How It Works

Each circle occupies one or two tables. Their published works are displayed with a sample copy (見本 — mihon) for browsing. Approach, look at the sample, and if you want to buy, indicate which item and pay the listed price.

Circle etiquette:

  • Don’t open or read a purchased book at the table — step aside first
  • Items without a price marked: ask “おいくらですか？” (how much?)
  • If there’s a queue post (for popular circles), join the designated queue — do not approach the table directly
  • Major circles may have volunteer queue staff managing a separate waiting area
  • Each person generally buys one copy; the creator sets their own purchase limits

Typical prices:

  • Small doujinshi (20–60 pages): ¥300–800
  • Standard doujinshi (60–200 pages): ¥800–1,500
  • Art books / luxury productions: ¥2,000–5,000+

R-18 Adult Works (成人向け)

Designated sections of certain halls contain age-restricted doujinshi. Black curtains (黒カーテン) mark the boundary.

  • Passport required — staff verify age at the R-18 area entrance
  • You must be 18 or older (Japan’s legal adult age)
  • Do not lift curtains or browse inside without intending to purchase
  • Separate from general circulation; follow the marked route

🏢 The Corporate Area

The Corporate Area (企業ブース) is a world of its own — a concentrated zone where major anime studios, game developers, figure makers, and publishers sell exclusive event-limited merchandise.

Strategy:

  1. Pre-event lottery (事前抽選): The most popular companies — Type-Moon, Key, Kadokawa, SEGA, Nitroplus, and others — run a pre-event lottery for their exclusive merchandise. Watch the company’s official website and social media from about 4–6 weeks before the event. Lottery registration deadlines are typically 2–4 weeks out. This is the only way to guarantee access to the hottest items.

  2. On-day wristband queues: On the day, many booths distribute numbered wristbands (整理券) starting at 7:00–8:00 outside the venue. Collect your wristband for your target booth first, then come back during your designated time window.

  3. Queue endurance: For non-wristband booths, expect 1–3 hours during peak morning hours. Queues for some flagship companies can be longer. Mid-afternoon (14:00–15:00) tends to be shorter — though popular items may be sold out.

  4. What’s sold: Event-exclusive figures, limited-edition art books, soundtrack CDs, merchandise collaborations, advance copies of upcoming releases, and signed goods.

📸 Cosplay at Comiket

Comiket has one of the world’s largest cosplay gatherings — thousands of cosplayers in elaborate costumes flood the outdoor plaza between halls on each day.

Rules for Cosplayers

  • You cannot enter the venue in costume. Arrive in street clothes and use the cosplay changing rooms (コスプレ着替え所) inside the venue. A fee applies (approx. ¥500–800 each way).
  • Costume rules: No realistic-looking weapons or firearms. Props must fit within specified dimensions. Large wings, tall constructions, and unconventional materials may require prior approval — check the detailed cosplay rules on the Comiket website well in advance of each edition.
  • Leaving the building: You must change back to street clothes before exiting the venue for good (entering the exit queue, taking transit home, etc.)
  • Cosplay outdoor area (コスプレ広場): The outdoor plaza between the East and West buildings is the designated area for costume photography. Wear your costume here.

Rules for Photographers

  • Always ask permission before photographing cosplayers. The standard phrase is “撮影いいですか？” (Satsuei ii desu ka? — “May I take a photo?"). Wait for a nod or “どうぞ” (please go ahead).
  • Photographing circle booth merchandise or page contents is not permitted without permission — buy it first.
  • Never follow cosplayers uninvited or photograph from behind without consent.
  • Delete photos immediately if asked — this is considered basic etiquette.

💴 Payments & Shipping Your Purchases

Cash First

ATMs inside and near the venue run dry by mid-morning on busy days. The 7-Eleven ATM network is the most reliable for international cards. Withdraw before the event day.

Yamato Transport Shipping (ヤマト運輸)

Comiket operates an official luggage forwarding service in partnership with Yamato Transport. A service counter inside the venue lets you box up your purchases and ship them directly to your hotel — arriving the following day — so you don’t have to carry everything on the train.

  • Costs: Approximately ¥1,000–2,000 per box depending on size and destination
  • Boxes: Available for purchase at the Yamato counter (¥50–200 each)
  • Process: Box your items, fill out a shipping slip (staff can help with Japanese), pay, receive a tracking number
  • Tip: Write your hotel’s address in Japanese — photocopy the hotel’s business card or booking confirmation

This service is strongly recommended for anyone buying more than they can comfortably carry in a tote bag.

Getting Purchases Home (International)

Direct international shipping from the Comiket venue is not available. Options:

  1. From your hotel: Major Tokyo hotels offer Japan Post EMS / SAL international parcel service — typically ¥2,000–5,000+ per parcel depending on weight and destination
  2. Tenso / Buyee forwarding service: Register before your trip to get a Japanese postal address. Ship purchases there, then forward to your home country. Costs extra but allows you to consolidate multiple parcels.
  3. Checked baggage: A full day’s Comiket haul can easily exceed 5–10 kg. Check airline baggage allowances before you go and consider purchasing additional baggage allowance in advance (much cheaper than at-airport fees).

🌡️ Surviving Summer Comiket — Heat Is the #1 Risk

⚠️ This is the single most important section of this guide for August attendees. Heatstroke at Comiket is not a hypothetical risk — it happens every single edition. Several attendees are hospitalised each summer, and most cases trace back to the same preventable mistakes: insufficient water, no electrolytes, denim clothing, and skipping breakfast.

Summer Comiket falls during Tokyo’s most brutal season. Temperatures of 34–38°C (93–100°F) combined with 70–80% humidity are the norm. The outdoor queue area — where attendees wait before the halls open — is exposed concrete with zero shade, and the radiant heat coming off it makes the feel-like temperature substantially higher than the air temperature.

💧 Hydration: The Single Most Important Habit

  • Drink before you’re thirsty. By the time you feel thirsty in 35°C heat, you are already dehydrated.
  • Carry at least 1.5 L of fluids before you join the queue — roughly half water, half electrolyte drink (Pocari Sweat / Aquarius).
  • Electrolytes matter more than water in extreme heat. Plain water alone can actually worsen heat illness through hyponatraemia (dangerously low sodium). Alternate sips: water → sports drink → water.
  • OS-1 (オーエスワン) is a medical-grade oral rehydration solution sold at every Japanese pharmacy and convenience store. Buy one before you head to the venue — keep it sealed as your emergency drink if you start to feel unwell.
  • Salt candy (塩飴, shio-ame) is sold at every convenience store in summer for ~¥200 a bag. Stash a handful in your pocket and chew one every 30–60 minutes while queuing.
  • Drink schedule while queuing: 200–300 ml every 20–30 minutes is the rough target. Set a phone timer if you tend to forget.
  • Refill stations: Vending machines inside the venue are abundant but queue. Convenience stores en route are fully stocked at 7 AM — top up before you arrive at Ariake.

👕 What to Wear

  • Lightweight, breathable, light-coloured fabrics — moisture-wicking sports materials are ideal
  • Quick-dry shirt — you will sweat through whatever you wear
  • 🚫 NEVER WEAR JEANS in August. This is the single most common mistake foreign visitors make. Denim does not breathe and traps heat against your legs — chino shorts, sports shorts, or quick-dry pants only.
  • Bring a spare shirt in your bag — changing into a dry shirt mid-day is a comfort upgrade you won’t regret
  • Avoid black clothing in the outdoor queue — it absorbs significantly more heat

🚨 Recognise Heatstroke Symptoms — Act Fast

Stage Symptoms What to do
Mild (heat exhaustion) Heavy sweating, mild dizziness, strong thirst, muscle cramps Move to shade or AC. Drink OS-1 or electrolyte drink. Cool neck with wet towel.
Moderate Headache, nausea, vomiting, weakness, confusion, rapid pulse Go to 救護室 (first aid) immediately — do not “tough it out”
Severe (heatstroke) Hot dry skin (sweating stopped), disorientation, seizures, loss of consciousness Dial 119 (Japanese emergency). Move to shade, douse with water. Life-threatening.

Every hall area has at least one first aid tent. Staff in green or orange vests can direct you. Medical staff at the first aid station are equipped to handle Comiket-volume heat cases — there is no shame in walking in. If you feel even slightly off, go.

🤝 Look Out for Each Other

If you’re attending with friends, check on each other every 30 minutes in the queue. Heatstroke can affect judgment before the sufferer realises they’re in trouble. Watch for: slurred speech, irritability, glassy eyes, stopping sweating despite the heat. Get them out of the sun immediately.

Note for Winter Comiket: The opposite problem applies — outdoor queues in late December can run 0–5°C before wind chill, with freezing wind coming off Tokyo Bay. Thermal base layers (HEATTECH), hand warmers (カイロ), a warm hat, and a thermos with hot tea or soup are essential. Hypothermia in long queues is real.

✅ Rules & Etiquette at a Glance

Always:

  • Follow queue staff (整理スタッフ) instructions without question
  • Keep moving steadily through hall walkways — don’t stop in the middle of traffic flow
  • Ask permission before photographing anyone, especially cosplayers
  • Stay to the left when walking (as per Japanese pedestrian convention)
  • Treat circle members with patience and courtesy — many appreciate foreign visitors

Never:

  • Cut lines — this is the most serious social violation at Comiket
  • Approach a circle table while a separate queue exists
  • Open purchased works at the circle table before stepping away
  • Bring large suitcases or protruding backpacks into hall circulation
  • Photograph circle merchandise, pages, or people without permission
  • Arrive at the venue in cosplay

🎯 First-Timer Strategy: How to Plan Your Day

1. Choose one day. Attending multiple days is exhausting on your first visit. Pick the day whose genre assignments match your interests.

2. Lock in your top 10 circles. Use the catalog app to build a route. Your absolute priorities get visited in the first 30 minutes — popular circles sell out fast.

3. Arrive at opening. If you have top-tier targets, aim to be in the queue by 8:00–9:00. For a more relaxed experience, arriving at 11:00–12:00 means shorter crowds but potentially sold-out targets.

4. Corporate Area mid-afternoon. Morning queues at popular corporate booths can run 2–3 hours. By 14:00 they often shorten significantly — though stock may be reduced.

5. Plan your exit. The final 90 minutes (approximately 14:30–16:00) bring the densest exit crowds. If your feet are finished by 14:00, you can often walk straight to the train without a queue. Waiting until closing means being swept along with 100,000+ people.

6. Akihabara after Comiket. Mandarake, K-Books, and Toranoana in Akihabara begin receiving resale copies of Comiket releases within 24–48 hours of the event. The evening after Day 3 and the morning of the following day are good hunting times for works you missed.

Comiket is demanding, disorienting, and unlike any event you have attended before. It is also one of the most extraordinary expressions of fan creativity anywhere in the world. With preparation, the right gear, and a willingness to navigate in Japanese, it rewards the effort handsomely — not just with rare books and exclusive merchandise, but with the unmistakable feeling of being at the centre of something genuinely unique.
