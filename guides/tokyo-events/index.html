Tokyo runs on a calendar of its own. In any given month, the city is hosting a centuries-old shrine festival, a national sumo tournament, a fireworks display drawing a million spectators, a pop-culture collaboration that exists only in 2026, and a seasonal spectacle timed to the blossoms or the autumn leaves. This guide gathers the events worth planning a 2026 trip around — the fixed annual traditions, the summer fireworks, and the one-off happenings you can only catch this year — with a month-by-month calendar at the end.

A note on dates: Confirmed 2026 dates are given where announced. Festival and firework dates can shift for weather or organiser decisions, and ticketed events sell out — always confirm on the official website before you build a day around one.

Summer Fireworks (Hanabi) — 2026 Dates

Tokyo’s fireworks season runs from late May through August, with several of Japan’s largest displays launching over the city’s rivers and stadiums. Arrive hours early for a free riverside spot, or buy a paid seat to avoid the crush.

Fireworks display 2026 date Notes
Adachi Fireworks (足立の花火) Late May Traditionally opens Tokyo’s fireworks season
Sumida River Fireworks (隅田川花火大会), 49th July 25 (Sat) Tokyo’s most famous; two venues near Sakurabashi and Komagatabashi, with Tokyo Skytree as backdrop
Katsushika Noryo Fireworks (葛飾納涼花火大会), 60th July 28 (Tue) ~20,000 shells, close launch distance, ~700,000 spectators
Edogawa-ku Fireworks (江戸川区花火大会), 51st August 1 (Sat) One of the metro area’s biggest
Itabashi Fireworks (いたばし花火大会) August 1 (Sat) Same night as Edogawa — you realistically pick one
Jingu Gaien Fireworks (神宮外苑花火大会) August 8 (Sat), rain date Aug 9 19:30–20:30, launched over Jingu Stadium and Chichibunomiya Rugby Ground, with live music; paid seating at both venues

Tips: The Sumida River display is the classic but the most crowded — the free viewing areas fill by early afternoon. For a calmer experience with a guaranteed view, the paid stadium seating at Jingu Gaien is the easiest to plan around. All are extremely hot and humid in July–August; bring water, a fan, and a hand towel.

Traditional Festivals (Matsuri)

Tokyo’s great Shinto festivals are Edo-era traditions that still shut down whole neighbourhoods. Two of the “three great festivals of Edo” have notable 2026 timing.

Sanja Matsuri (三社祭) — May 15–17, 2026

The grand annual festival of Asakusa Shrine, and Tokyo’s rowdiest. Over three days, around a hundred mikoshi (portable shrines) are carried through Asakusa by teams of chanting bearers, drawing roughly 1.5 million people to the streets around Sensoji Temple. The Saturday and Sunday are the peak; the atmosphere is dense, loud, and unforgettable. Free to watch — just join the crowds around Kaminarimon and the Nakamise shopping street.

Sanno Matsuri (山王祭) — June 7–17, 2026 ⭐ Grand-festival year

One of the three great festivals of Edo, held by Hie Shrine in Akasaka. 2026 is a “hon-matsuri” (grand festival) year — the full-scale version happens only every two years (even years), so this is the one to catch. The highlight is the Jinkosai, a 300-metre procession of costumed participants and ornate palanquins winding through central Tokyo, from Akasaka past the Imperial Palace. If you want to see the large-scale Edo procession, plan for the mid-June dates.

Kanda Matsuri (神田祭) — 2026 is a “kage-matsuri” (quiet year)

Kanda Shrine’s famous festival alternates: the massive procession happens in odd-numbered years (2025, 2027). In 2026 it is a “kage-matsuri," a scaled-down year centred on quieter religious rites without the huge public parade. Worth knowing so you’re not expecting the full spectacle.

Other traditional summer festivals to watch for include Torigoe Shrine’s heavy mikoshi festival (June), the Fukagawa Hachiman Festival’s water-throwing (August), and countless neighbourhood bon-odori dances throughout July and August.

Grand Sumo Tournaments (Ryogoku Kokugikan)

Three of the six annual Grand Sumo tournaments are held in Tokyo, at the Ryogoku Kokugikan. Each runs 15 days. Tickets range from arena seats to traditional ringside box seats, and popular days sell out — buy through the official channel well ahead.

Tournament 2026 dates
January (Hatsu-basho) Jan 11–25
May (Natsu-basho) May 10–24
September (Aki-basho) Sep 13–27

For visitors, sumo is one of Tokyo’s most rewarding cultural experiences — arrive by mid-afternoon to catch the lower-division bouts, then stay for the top-division ring-entering ceremony and the marquee matches around 5–6pm.

Pop Culture & One-Off 2026 Events

These are the happenings unique to 2026 — the reason to time a trip this year specifically.

Chiikawa × Tokyo Skytree — from July 10, 2026

Tied to the film Chiikawa: Secret of Mermaid Island (opening July 24, 2026), Tokyo Skytree hosts “Chiikawa: Star-Falling Skytree and the Secret Island” (ちいかわ☆星ふるスカイツリーとひみつの島) — the wildly popular character franchise’s first Skytree collaboration in three years. From July 10, the Tembo Deck, Tembo Galleria (450m up), and the 1F “SKYTREE SPACE” feature themed decorations, a photo spot with the Chiikawa characters, a participatory star-magnet message board, a limited-edition character café menu, and exclusive event goods. A must for Chiikawa fans and a fun stop for families.

Comiket — the world’s largest doujinshi fair

Held at Tokyo Big Sight. 2026 has two editions:

  • Comiket 108 (C108): August 15–16, 2026 — part of Comiket’s 50th-anniversary run. Note the East 4–6 halls are closed for renovation, and opening time shifts to 10:30. Expect extreme summer heat in the queues.
  • Comiket 109 (C109): December 29–31, 2026 — the traditional three-day year-end edition.

See our dedicated Comiket survival guide for queuing, what to bring, and access tips.

teamLab Borderless special exhibition — Azabudai Hills

The digital-art museum teamLab Borderless at Azabudai Hills continues to draw huge crowds (over 4 million visitors since opening). Through 2026 it has staged special additions such as On the Asymmetry of the Universe (shown July–October 2026), introducing new artwork series alongside the permanent world. Book timed-entry tickets online in advance — walk-up slots routinely sell out.

Major museum exhibitions (2026)

Tokyo’s 2026 exhibition calendar is exceptional, with several international blockbusters. The headline shows to plan around:

The Gaudí centennial show at Terrada Warehouse is a highlight: to mark 100 years since the architect’s death, it presents original Sagrada Família drawings, Gaudí’s own notebooks and letters, and his working tools — reportedly the first time so many pieces from the archive have been shown together — wrapped in an immersive digital production by the art collective NAKED. The National Art Center in Roppongi alone runs four major shows across the year, ending with the Louvre’s Renaissance exhibition in autumn. Book online in advance for the international blockbusters, which draw long lines on weekends; confirm exact dates and hours on each venue’s official site, as some are announced closer to opening.

Cherry Blossom Season (Spring 2026)

Tokyo’s cherry blossoms are an event in themselves. The 2026 forecast points to first bloom around March 21 and full bloom in late March, though the exact window shifts each year with the weather.

  • Meguro River — canal lined with cherries, lit in the evening
  • Ueno Park — the classic, boisterous hanami picnic scene
  • Chidorigafuchi (Imperial Palace moat) — rowboats beneath the blossoms, evening illumination
  • Shinjuku Gyoen — many varieties, a calmer paid-entry garden
  • Yasukuni Shrine & Sumida Park — Tokyo’s benchmark trees and riverside rows

Peak bloom lasts only about a week. Track the Japan Meteorological Corporation forecast in the weeks before your trip and stay flexible.

Autumn Leaves (Late Nov–Early Dec)

Tokyo’s foliage peaks noticeably later than the countryside — usually late November into early December.

  • Meiji Jingu Gaien Ginkgo Avenue — the golden ginkgo tunnel, Tokyo’s signature autumn image
  • Rikugien Garden — evening maple illumination, one of the finest in the city
  • Koishikawa Korakuen & Kyu-Shiba-rikyu — classic strolling gardens in full colour
  • Mt. Takao (western Tokyo) — day-trip hiking with mountain foliage

Winter Illuminations (Nov 2025–Feb 2026 season, and again from late 2026)

From November through February, Tokyo glows. Major displays running into early 2026 include:

  • Roppongi — Midtown Christmas — the Midtown Garden light-and-sound show and Keyakizaka Avenue
  • Marunouchi Illumination — champagne-gold trees along the avenues near Tokyo Station
  • Shibuya Blue Cave (Ao no Dokutsu) — a tunnel of blue light in Yoyogi Park / Shibuya
  • Yomiuriland Jewellumination — a theme-park display of roughly 6.5 million LEDs (ran Oct 2025–early April 2026)
  • Tokyo Mega Illumi (Oi Racecourse) and department-store and mall displays citywide

The same displays return from late 2026 for the 2026–2027 winter season.

New Year in Tokyo (Late Dec – Early Jan)

  • Hatsumode (first shrine visit) — Meiji Jingu draws around three million visitors over the first three days; Sensoji in Asakusa and Kanda Myojin are also major. Expect long, orderly queues.
  • Joya-no-Kane — temple bells rung 108 times on New Year’s Eve
  • First sunrise (hatsuhinode) viewing from high spots and the bay
  • Emperor’s New Year Greeting — the Imperial Palace opens for public greetings on January 2
  • Countdown events at Shibuya, Zojoji Temple (balloon release), and venues citywide

Practical Tips

  • Book accommodation early for peak dates. Hotels around the Sumida Fireworks (late July), Comiket (mid-Aug, late Dec), and cherry blossom season (late March) fill and jump in price months ahead.
  • Confirm every date on the official site. Firework and festival dates move for weather; ticketed events (sumo, paid firework seating, teamLab) sell out.
  • Summer means heat. July–August events are hot and humid — water, electrolytes, a hat, and a hand fan are essentials, especially in firework and Comiket queues.
  • Festivals are free; arrive early. For Sanja, Sanno, and the fireworks, the best free viewing spots go hours before start time.
  • Respect the crowds and the rites. At shrine festivals keep to the spectator areas, don’t block mikoshi teams, and follow staff directions. Note that Shibuya actively discourages unofficial Halloween gatherings and street drinking around October 31 — there is no official event.
