Sumida River Fireworks Guide: Tokyo's Greatest Summer Spectacle

Last updated: June 20, 2026

The Sumida River Fireworks Festival (隅田川花火大会, Sumidagawa Hanabi Taikai) is the oldest and most prestigious fireworks festival in Tokyo, with roots tracing back to 1733. Over 20,000 shells launch across two competing sites along the Sumida River on the last Saturday of July, watched by roughly one million spectators packed onto bridges, riverbanks, rooftops, and into restaurants for miles around. It is one of the definitive Tokyo summer experiences — and also one of the most logistically demanding to attend well.

This guide is built around a single truth: the fireworks are spectacular, but the crowd is the real challenge. Plan for the crowd, and you’ll have a wonderful evening. Ignore it, and you’ll spend three hours unable to move and two more hours unable to get home.

🗓️ Quick Reference
When Last Saturday of July, ~19:00–20:30
Where Two sites along the Sumida River, Asakusa area
Cost to watch Free (public areas) / ¥3,000–60,000+ (paid seating)
Shells launched ~20,000 over 90 minutes
Attendance ~1,000,000
Rain policy Held in light rain; cancelled (not postponed) if severe
Nearest stations Asakusa, Kuramae, Oshiage (Skytree), Ryogoku

The Two Launch Sites

The festival is unusual in running two separate launch sites simultaneously, which historically competed against each other (a tradition dating from rival fireworks guilds).

  • Site 1 (第一会場): Between Sakurabashi and Kototoibashi bridges. Launches ~9,350 shells, including the competitive “fireworks design contest” segment featuring new and experimental shells from Japan’s top pyrotechnic companies.
  • Site 2 (第二会場): Between Komagatabashi and Umayabashi bridges, closer to Asakusa. Launches ~10,650 shells. Generally easier to reach from Asakusa Station.

The two sites are about 1km apart. You generally cannot see both well from a single location — pick one and commit.

Where to Watch for Free

Free viewing requires arriving extremely early (the best public spots are claimed by early afternoon for an evening show) and accepting dense crowds. The main free options:

Sumida Park (Both Banks)

The riverside park stretches along both banks near Asakusa and is the most popular free viewing area. Spots are claimed with leisure sheets from as early as 1–2pm. By 4pm the prime riverside positions are full. By 6pm the entire park is at capacity and entry may be restricted by police for crowd safety.

Komagata Bridge / Umaya Bridge Area

Closer to Site 2. Bridges themselves are designated “no stopping” zones during the festival (police keep people moving for safety) so you cannot stand on the bridges to watch — but the approaches and nearby streets offer partial views.

Further Afern: Free Distant Views

For a far less crowded experience, accept a more distant view. Areas that catch the fireworks above the skyline without the riverside crush:

  • Higashi-Komagata and Honjo neighbourhoods (east side, residential streets)
  • Oshiage / Tokyo Skytree base area — the fireworks appear alongside the Skytree, a remarkable combination, though buildings partially block lower bursts
  • Riverside spots downstream near Ryogoku — smaller crowds, fireworks visible at distance

Paid Seating: The Civilised Option

For visitors who want to actually enjoy the evening without a 5-hour endurance test, paid seating is strongly recommended. Options:

Official Paid Viewing Areas

The festival organisers sell tickets to designated riverside viewing zones (typically announced and sold in May–June). These guarantee a spot but sell out quickly and require advance Japanese-language application in many cases.

Restaurant & Rooftop Packages

Many restaurants, hotels, and venues near the river sell fireworks-viewing dinner packages. These range from ¥8,000 izakaya packages to ¥30,000–60,000 hotel rooftop experiences with food and drink included.

  • Asakusa View Hotel and similar high-floor venues sell fireworks dinner plans
  • Yakatabune (traditional roofed boats) offer dinner cruises on the river during the festival — an atmospheric (if expensive) option at ¥20,000–30,000 per person; book months in advance
  • Tokyo Skytree sells special fireworks-viewing tickets for its observation decks — a unique above-the-fireworks perspective

Booking timeline

Restaurant and boat packages for the last-Saturday-of-July festival often open booking in May and sell out by late June. If you want guaranteed comfortable viewing, book early.

Crowd & Timing Strategy

This is the most important section of the guide.

If watching for free:

  • Arrive at your chosen spot by 2–3pm to claim ground with a leisure sheet
  • Bring everything you need for a 5–6 hour wait (food, drink, fan, portable charger, entertainment)
  • Designate one person to hold the spot while others explore Asakusa
  • Accept that you will not be able to move freely once the show begins

If you have paid seating:

  • Follow your venue’s specified arrival time (usually 1–2 hours before the show)

The post-festival exodus is the real ordeal. When the fireworks end at ~20:30, one million people attempt to reach the same handful of train stations simultaneously. Asakusa, Oshiage, and Kuramae stations implement crowd-control measures: entry queues that can take 60–90 minutes to even reach the platform.

Exit strategies:

  1. Leave 10 minutes early. Sacrifice the finale to beat the crush. Many experienced attendees do this.
  2. Wait it out. Stay in the park or find a restaurant for 60–90 minutes after the show, let the crowd disperse, then walk to a station.
  3. Walk to a farther station. Instead of the nearest (jammed) station, walk 20–25 minutes to a less crowded line — e.g. walk south to Kuramae or further to Ryogoku.

⚠️ Summer Heat & Comfort Preparation

Late July in Tokyo is brutally hot and humid: 30–35°C with 70–80% humidity, and the evening barely cools. A 5-hour wait in a dense, sun-exposed crowd is a genuine heat-exhaustion risk.

Essential preparation:

  • Water and electrolytes: Bring more than you think you need. Pocari Sweat or OS-1 (oral rehydration) from any convenience store. Vending machines near the festival sell out and have long queues.
  • Handheld fan: A battery-powered handheld fan or a traditional uchiwa/sensu fan is close to mandatory.
  • Cooling sheets: Convenience stores sell “Hiyaron” cold packs and cooling body sheets (冷却シート) — apply to neck and forehead.
  • Sun protection: For afternoon arrival, a hat and sunscreen. The sun is intense until ~6:30pm.
  • Eat before and bring food: Don’t skip meals. Convenience store onigiri, snacks, and drinks are the standard festival meal. Buy them before reaching the river — local shops sell out.
  • Portable phone charger: Networks get congested with a million people; your battery drains fast searching for signal.

Recognise heatstroke: Dizziness, nausea, stopping sweating, confusion. If you or someone in your group shows these signs, move to shade/AC immediately, hydrate, and seek the festival’s first-aid stations (located on event maps and staffed by medical personnel).

Wearing Yukata: The Authentic Summer Look

The Sumida fireworks are one of the great yukata occasions of the year. A yukata is a light cotton summer kimono, and wearing one to a hanabi festival is entirely appropriate and encouraged for visitors.

Where to get one:

  • Rental shops in Asakusa offer yukata rental with dressing service for ¥3,000–6,000, including obi belt and accessories. Many open early on festival day. Book ahead for the last Saturday of July — they fill up.
  • Buy a set: Inexpensive yukata sets (yukata + obi + geta sandals) are sold at Uniqlo, Don Quijote, and department stores in summer for ¥3,000–8,000.

Practical yukata tips for the festival:

  • Wear comfortable footwear if walking far — traditional geta sandals cause blisters over long distances and standing
  • The crowd and heat make a full traditional outfit hot; modern light yukata fabrics help
  • A small drawstring bag (kinchaku) is the traditional accessory for carrying essentials

What Else to Do in Asakusa Beforehand

Since you’ll likely arrive in the early afternoon, Asakusa offers a full day before the show:

  • Senso-ji Temple and Nakamise shopping street (see our Senso-ji guide)
  • Street food on the festival approach: yakitori, takoyaki, yakisoba, kakigori (shaved ice), chocolate bananas, and candy apples from yatai stalls
  • Hoppy Street izakaya for an early drink
  • Tokyo Skytree across the river (book ahead — it’s busy on festival day)

If It Rains

The Sumida River Fireworks Festival is held in light rain. In the event of severe weather (heavy rain, lightning, typhoon), the festival is cancelled outright — not postponed to another day. There is no rain date.

Check the official festival website and Japanese weather forecasts on the day. If cancellation is announced (usually by midday), have a backup plan for the evening.

Getting There

Closest stations (extremely crowded):

  • Asakusa Station (Ginza Line, Asakusa Line, Tobu Skytree Line) — for Site 2 and Sumida Park west bank
  • Oshiage Station / Tokyo Skytree (Hanzomon Line, Tobu Skytree Line) — for east bank and Skytree views
  • Kuramae Station (Asakusa Line, Oedo Line) — slightly less crowded alternative
  • Ryogoku Station (Sobu Line, Oedo Line) — downstream, for distant viewing with smaller crowds

Arrive early, leave late (or very early). Station crowd control means the train journey to and from the festival is part of the experience to plan for, not an afterthought.
