Aoi Matsuri Guide: Kyoto's Elegant Heian Procession

Last updated: June 20, 2026

The Aoi Matsuri is the most elegant and aristocratic of Kyoto’s three great festivals — a serene procession that recreates the courtly world of a thousand years ago. On May 15 each year, more than 500 people in exquisite Heian-period costume, accompanied by horses, ox-drawn carts, and decorations of aoi (hollyhock) leaves, walk in a slow, dignified parade from the Kyoto Imperial Palace to the city’s two ancient Kamo shrines. Without the noise and crowds of summer’s Gion Matsuri, Aoi Matsuri is a living scroll painting — gentle, graceful, and deeply historic. This guide covers what it is, the procession, its history, and how to watch.

One of Kyoto’s Three Great Festivals

Aoi Matsuri (the “Hollyhock Festival”) is, with Gion Matsuri and Jidai Matsuri, one of Kyoto’s three major festivals — and the oldest, with roots reaching back over 1,400 years to the 6th century. It originated as a ritual to appease the gods of the Kamo shrines after storms and failed harvests, and by the Heian period had become a grand imperial event woven into court life — it features memorably in classical literature like The Tale of Genji. Its formal name is the Kamo Matsuri, but it’s known as Aoi Matsuri for the hollyhock (aoi) leaves that adorn the participants, carts, and oxen, once believed to ward off natural disasters.

The Procession

The heart of the festival is the Roto-no-gi, the procession of over 500 people in full Heian court costume:

  • Imperial messenger & nobles on horseback in formal dress.
  • Ox-drawn carts (gosho-guruma): Beautiful lacquered carriages decorated with flowers and hollyhock — the iconic image of the festival.
  • The Saio-dai: The festival’s female star — a young woman chosen to represent the imperial princess who once served the Kamo shrines, carried in a stunning twelve-layered junihitoe kimono, surrounded by attendants. The Saio-dai and her ladies are the most photographed sight.
  • Horses, retainers, musicians, and porters, all in period attire, with the whole parade dressed in hollyhock leaves.

The procession is slow, silent but for hooves and the creak of carts — a deliberately stately recreation of the past.

The Route

The parade sets out from the Kyoto Imperial Palace (Gosho) in the morning (around 10:30 am), proceeds north to Shimogamo Shrine for rituals around midday, then continues in the afternoon to Kamigamo Shrine, arriving in the later afternoon. Both shrines are ancient, forested, UNESCO-listed sites well worth visiting in their own right.

History You’re Watching

When you watch Aoi Matsuri you’re seeing a window into Heian aristocratic ceremony, the refined court culture that produced Japan’s classical literature and arts. The festival lapsed at times through history and was revived; today it is faithfully maintained as a precious piece of living heritage — less a party than a solemn, beautiful pageant.

Best Viewing

  • Along the route: You can watch for free anywhere along the Imperial Palace → Shimogamo → Kamigamo route. The leafy path through the Tadasu-no-mori forest approaching Shimogamo Shrine is an especially atmospheric spot.
  • Paid grandstand seats are sold at the Imperial Palace and Shimogamo Shrine for a comfortable, guaranteed view — recommended if you want to sit and see the whole procession well.
  • Arrive early to claim a good roadside position before the procession passes.
  • Date note: Held on May 15; if it rains heavily it may be postponed to the next day — check on the day.

Combining Your Visit

  • Visit the two Kamo shrines themselves — Shimogamo (with its primeval Tadasu-no-mori forest) and Kamigamo — among Kyoto’s oldest and most sacred sites.
  • May is a glorious time in Kyoto — fresh green everywhere; combine with riverside walks along the Kamo River by bike.
  • For Kyoto’s other festivals, see our Kyoto festivals guide, Gion Matsuri, and Jidai Matsuri.

Getting There

  • Imperial Palace start: Marutamachi or Imadegawa stations (Karasuma subway line).
  • Shimogamo Shrine: Near Demachiyanagi (Keihan/Eizan lines).
  • Kamigamo Shrine: By city bus to the north. Pick a spot along the route that fits your day and arrive ahead of the procession’s timing.
